  • My undergraduate capstone at Trinity College Dublin, in Prof. Cormac McGuinness's Surface, Interface Physics & X-ray Spectroscopy Group. The aim was to find the band gap of metal-porphyrin chains and where their HOMO and LUMO states are located, using density functional theory in FHI-aims.

    The project found that the carbon bridge between unit cells in the 1D chain can change the HOMO-LUMO gap, where the frontier states are located and whether the chain is magnetic. In zinc chains a fully fused bridge carries the HOMO along the whole chain, while a weak bridge leaves it on each unit. With iron and vanadium centres the metal's d orbitals move into the gap and shorten it, so the frontier states are on the metal. Weakly bonded chains carry unpaired electrons on their carbon backbone, while more strongly fused chains tend to pair them up. The simulations point to the weak bridge keeping each unit's orbitals separate, so each porphyrin keeps the unpaired electrons it has as a single molecule, while fusing joins them into bands along the chain.

    Furthermore, it was found that an oxygen bound to the central metal can override the effect of the carbon bridge. The backbone spin disappears, leaving a single local spin on the metal-oxygen unit (S = 1 for iron, S = ½ for vanadium), and the HOMO-LUMO gap widens, from 0.34 to 0.64 eV in the fully fused iron chain.

    This points towards potential for chemical sensors and molecular electronics, both of great interest with porphyrins (Paolesse et al., 2017), given the graphene-like conduction that fused porphyrin chains can display (Gao et al., 2023).

    Porphyrins are flat rings of four pyrroles that hold a metal atom at the centre. They are the core of haemoglobin (iron) and chlorophyll (magnesium), and their spin and charge can be tuned through the central metal and whatever binds to it, which makes them candidates for molecular electronics, catalysis and chemical sensing. This project came about because on-surface synthesis can now make more complex porphyrin chains and ribbons, and it carries on from earlier studies of these larger porphyrin systems.

    The calculations ran in FHI-aims, the Fritz Haber Institute's density functional theory code, which expands the electron orbitals in numeric atom-centred functions. DFT replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density n(r) and solves the Kohn-Sham equations self-consistently, once for each spin channel:

    [ −½∇² + v_ext(r) + v_H[n](r) + v_xc[n](r) ] ψᵢ(r) = εᵢ ψᵢ(r), n(r) = Σᵢ |ψᵢ(r)|²

    Three design choices were varied: the carbon bridge between units (weak, strong or fully fused), the fused phenyl arms (none, four or two) and the central metal (zinc, iron, vanadium, cobalt and manganese, spanning the d shell from three electrons to ten). The main results:

    • The carbon bridge changes the gap and where the frontier states are located. In zinc chains the weak bridge leaves the HOMO on each unit and the fully fused bridge carries it along the whole chain. The gap is 0.31 eV weak, 0.05 eV strong and 0.78 eV fully fused, so more fusion narrows it only up to a point.
    • The metal's d orbitals influence the HOMO and LUMO. Zinc's full d shell leaves the carbon backbone on its own. In iron and vanadium chains the d levels lie among the carbon levels and shorten the gap, so the frontier states are on the metal. Manganese gives single-molecule gaps close to iron's (0.24 to 0.30 eV), while the cobalt centre had little effect on the density of states. A metal state at the edge of the gap, one per unit, points to electrons hopping from metal to metal rather than moving along a band, which would change how the chain conducts.
    • Oxidising the central metal fixes the spin. With one oxygen bound to the metal (Fe=O or V=O), the spin no longer changes with the bridge. Every iron chain has two unpaired electrons (total spin S = 1), shared between the iron and the oxygen, and every vanadium chain has one (S = ½), on the vanadium. The backbone spin, meaning unpaired spin on the chain's carbon, disappears. The gap widens and runs from about 0.15 eV (strong bridge) to 0.64 eV (fully fused).
    • The bridge structure also affects the magnetism of bare chains, meaning chains with no oxygen on the metal. With iron or vanadium centres, the weak bridge leaves about 2 μB of unpaired spin on the carbon backbone, and with the strong bridge the backbone carries almost none.
    • Cobalt and manganese chains did not converge. 6 of 45 cobalt runs finished and none of the seven manganese chains, so the chain results are for zinc, iron and vanadium. The likely reason is that both metals have several spin states close in energy, so the self-consistent loop kept switching between them instead of settling. Cobalt is also a hard case in experiment, where Sun et al. found its spin in PorA₂ screened by the gold surface (a Kondo effect).

    The gap and the frontier states are set by the bridge and the metal, and the spin by the metal-oxygen unit, so each could be tuned separately.

    1. 01Simulate a single molecule first

      We start with DFT simulations of a single porphyrin molecule, the unit the chains are built from. We run the plain ring (ZnP), then the ring with four fused phenyl arms (ZnTPP) and with two (ZnDPP). Zinc comes first because its full d shell has no unpaired electron, so the orbitals we see belong to the carbon framework alone.

      The plain ring is the form found in haem and chlorophyll. The phenyl arms are what on-surface synthesis fuses onto the ring when the molecules are heated on gold, and these are the molecules Sun et al. (2020) made and measured, as PorA₄ and PorA₂. Fusing the arms joins their carbon π orbitals to the ring's, so the molecule becomes a small piece of graphene-like carbon. This extended π system is what can carry charge along a chain.

      The figure shows HOMO−2 and LUMO+2 of each molecule, red and blue for the two signs of the wavefunction. On the plain ring the orbitals are on the pyrroles, and with the arms fused they spread out over the arms.

      The gaps show the main result. ZnP has a 2.77 eV gap and ZnTPP 1.17 eV, while ZnDPP's collapses to 0.10 eV. In a calculation without spin, every orbital must hold two electrons, one spin up and one spin down. A molecule with two unpaired electrons cannot be described that way, so the calculation shares the two electrons across two orbitals of almost the same energy, each half full, and the gap between those orbitals comes out close to zero. A near-zero gap in a run without spin is therefore the sign of two unpaired electrons, the open-shell PorA₂ that Sun et al. measured.

      Settings. PBE exchange-correlation functional, collinear spin where the metal has unpaired electrons, FHI-aims' numeric atom-centred basis, and every run converged to 10⁻⁵ in the density and 10⁻⁵ eV in the total energy. The chains are one unit cell repeated along the chain axis, sampled with 30 k-points.

    2. 02Swap in magnetic transition metals

      Next the zinc is replaced by transition metals with unpaired d electrons: iron, manganese, cobalt and vanadium. Each molecule is run spin-polarised, with separate spin-up and spin-down densities.

      The spin moment measures the unpaired electrons, the part of the molecule that is magnetic. Splitting the simulated spin into the part on the metal and the part on the ring (Mulliken analysis) shows whether the magnetism stays on the metal or spreads into the carbon that would carry a current in a chain. The gap is the energy needed to lift an electron into the first empty state, so a smaller gap points to easier conduction.

      The top panel shows the HOMO-LUMO gap for every metal and arm structure, on a log scale because the gaps run from 0.04 to 3.3 eV. Apart from cobalt's plain ring, every magnetic metal brings the gap down to between 0.04 and 0.34 eV, against 1.17 to 2.77 eV for zinc's closed-shell molecules. The bottom panels show the spin on the metal (green) and on the ring (purple) for each arm structure, joined by a line. Metal by metal:

      • Iron. Gap 0.04 eV plain, 0.34 eV with two arms, 0.30 eV with four. With two arms the ring picks up 1.9 μB aligned with the metal's 2.1, 4 μB in total. With four arms the ring spin points against the metal and the total cancels to zero.
      • Manganese. The largest metal moment, about 3.5 μB. The ring carries 1.4 μB aligned with two arms and 2.3 μB against with four. Gaps 0.24 to 0.30 eV.
      • Cobalt. Gap 3.33 eV on the plain ring, the widest of the magnetic metals, and 0.25 eV with four arms. The two-arm run did not finish.
      • Vanadium. Gaps 0.19 to 0.24 eV, the narrowest. With two arms little spin reaches the ring (0.2 μB), and with four the ring carries 2 μB aligned with the metal.
      • Zinc (no spin, for reference). Gaps 2.77, 1.17 and 0.10 eV.

      Sun et al. (2022) measured iron in PorA₂ on gold at about 3.7 μB, with the ring and metal spins aligned, and this run gives 4 μB, aligned. The four-arm result has not been measured, and PBE is known to overstate this kind of spin polarisation, so it is the less certain of the two.

    3. 03Orbitals molecule by molecule

      Every combination of metal and arms was run, which took a lot of numerical, convergence and speed tuning. This figure shows the standouts, five molecules with different metals and arm structures, and it is where the metals for the chains were chosen.

      For each one we look at the four orbitals around the gap: HOMO−1, HOMO, LUMO and LUMO+1. The HOMO holds the highest-energy electron and the LUMO is the first empty state an added electron would go into, so charge enters and leaves the molecule through these orbitals, and a current would pass through them. The question for each orbital is whether it is located on the metal or on the carbon. When the HOMO and LUMO are both on the carbon, the carbon π system sets the gap and the metal is a bystander. When one of them is on the metal, the metal's d levels have pushed in among the carbon levels, so the metal sets the gap, and changing the metal or what binds to it would tune it.

      The renders are from the thesis, red and blue for the two signs of the wavefunction. Row by row:

      • Zn-TPP. All four orbitals are spread over the ring and the four arms, with almost nothing on the zinc. This is the carbon-only reference.
      • Fe-TPP. The HOMO is a single round lobe on the iron with nothing on the carbon around it. Side-on it is iron's dz² orbital standing out of the ring plane. HOMO−1, LUMO and LUMO+1 are on the carbon, so iron's filled d level lies inside the carbon gap.
      • Fe-DPP. The same iron HOMO, with small lobes on the four nitrogens. The carbon orbitals around it stretch along the molecule's long axis, the direction the two fused arms point.
      • V-TPP. All four orbitals are on the ring and arms, as with zinc, but the vanadium joins in, with a lobe at the centre in HOMO−1 and LUMO.
      • V-DPP. The orbitals are on the ring and extend to the vanadium through the nitrogens, again stretched along the long axis.

      Iron gives the clearest case of a metal level inside the carbon gap, the most direct handle for tuning. Vanadium mixes metal and carbon. A mixed state means the metal's d orbital and the carbon π orbitals have combined into one orbital, so an electron in it is shared between the metal and the ring. The metal is then coupled into the carbon network that carries charge, which points towards band-like conduction through the metal rather than hopping from one metal to the next, and the thesis found the vanadium chains promising for charge conduction for this reason. Zinc is the carbon-only baseline. These three are the metals followed into the chains below.

    4. 04Linear porphyrin chains

      The molecules are then joined into linear chains. Each chain is simulated as one unit cell repeated along the chain axis, sampled with 30 k-points, and run spin-polarised for iron and vanadium. Three bridges were used. A weak bridge is a single carbon-carbon bond between arms, a strong bridge is three bonds after adding two carbons, and in the fully fused (extra-strong) chain the arms merge, so the backbone becomes a narrow graphene-like ribbon. A fourth chain joins two-arm (DPP) units end to end. These were the bridges partly or fully made by on-surface synthesis at the time.

      The orbitals are read by where they are located along the chain. Electrons can move smoothly along orbitals that run unbroken along the chain, as in an energy band, so a HOMO or LUMO spread over the whole backbone points to a chain that can carry current. An orbital that stays on each unit, or on each metal, means an electron has to hop from unit to unit, and the chain conducts poorly through that state. The gap is the energy needed to get an electron into the empty band.

      The figure shows the zinc chains' HOMO and LUMO, zinc first so the backbone can be seen on its own:

      • Weak bridge, gap 0.31 eV. The HOMO is on each porphyrin separately and breaks at the bridge, while the LUMO crosses it. An added electron could move along the chain through the LUMO, but a hole in the HOMO could not.
      • Strong bridge, gap 0.05 eV. The smallest gap of any chain. The HOMO stays on the outer arms of each unit, and the LUMO runs continuously through the bridges, so this is the zinc chain closest to conducting.
      • Fully fused, gap 0.78 eV. The HOMO runs the whole length of the chain without a break, a band along the ribbon. The gap opens again, as narrow graphene ribbons are known to do, so more fusion gives a smaller gap only up to a point.

      Then the magnetic metals go into the same chains (bare chains, spin-polarised):

      • Iron. Gap 0.21 eV weak, 0.13 eV strong, 0.34 eV fully fused and 0.29 eV DPP. The HOMO is the iron's dz² orbital, one lobe per unit with nothing linking them, and the LUMO is on the carbon.
      • Vanadium. Gap 0.18 eV weak and 0.22 eV DPP. In the weak chain the LUMO is a d orbital on each vanadium and the LUMO+1 is on the carbon rings. In the strong chain the HOMO is on the vanadium, and in the DPP chain the HOMO and LUMO are both on the vanadium, with the backbone barely involved (second figure).

      The main finding of this step is that in both iron and vanadium chains the metal's d orbital is at the frontier, inside the carbon gap. The first states an electron would enter or leave are on the metal and separate on each unit, and the carbon states that could carry a current lie just beyond them. The metal therefore sets the gap and the spin at the frontier, and changing what is bound to the metal is the way to tune them, which the oxygen test below does.

      The bare iron chains are the least certain numbers here. Their spin settled differently from chain to chain, so the result may depend on where each calculation started, and PBE is known to be unreliable for spin states in iron porphyrins.

    5. 05Orbitals along the chains

      This figure puts the HOMO and LUMO of four chains side by side, five units of each, to compare where the frontier orbitals are located once metals are in the chain:

      • Zinc, strong bridge. The HOMO is on each unit's pyrroles and arms and breaks at every bridge. The LUMO runs continuously through the bridges, a band along the whole chain, and this is the conducting state.
      • Iron, DPP chain. The HOMO is a row of iron dz² lobes, one per unit, with nothing in between, so a hole would stay on one iron. The LUMO is spread over the carbon of every unit and joins up between them.
      • Vanadium, weak bridge. The HOMO is a d orbital on each vanadium with some weight on the nitrogens around it. The LUMO is on the carbon rings of each unit, linked only through the single bond.
      • Vanadium, fully fused. The HOMO and LUMO are both on the vanadium, so the ribbon's carbon plays no part at the frontier.

      Zinc is the only chain with a continuous band at the frontier. Iron and vanadium put localised metal states at the edge of the gap, one per unit, with the carbon conduction states one step away. The metal states can be moved by binding something to the metal without rebuilding the backbone, and the next step does this with a single oxygen.

    6. 06Oxidising the central metal

      The last test came from the thesis conclusion, that these chains could be used for chemical sensing if a spin could be imposed through a ligand on the metal. Every iron and vanadium chain was run again with a single oxygen bound to the metal, 1.6 Å below the ring, which oxidises it to iron-oxo (Fe=O) or vanadyl (V=O). The question is what the oxygen does to the spin and to the gap.

      The figure shows the spin moment per unit cell of every chain, split into the part on the metal (green), the oxygen (grey) and the carbon backbone (purple). Faded bars are the bare chains from the previous steps and solid bars are the same chains with the oxygen bound.

      Without oxygen the spin is different in every chain. The weak iron chain has 1.8 μB on its carbon backbone, opposite to the metal, the weak vanadium chain has 2 μB on its backbone, aligned with the metal, and the chains with other bridges have almost none. The magnetism of a bare chain depends on the bridge, and for iron possibly on how the calculation was started. Oxidising the metal removes those differences:

      • Iron (Fe=O). 2 μB in every chain, 1.2 on the iron and 0.8 on the oxygen, the S = 1 known for iron(IV)-oxo porphyrins. Iron shares its spin with the oxygen.
      • Vanadium (V=O). 1 μB in every chain, almost all of it on the vanadium, the S = ½ of a vanadyl porphyrin. The oxygen takes almost no spin (−0.13 μB).
      • Backbone. The spin on the carbon backbone falls below 0.08 μB in every chain.
      • Gap. The gap does not collapse. It widens in every chain (second figure), for iron from 0.21 to 0.25 eV (weak), 0.13 to 0.15 eV (strong), 0.34 to 0.64 eV (fully fused) and 0.29 to 0.34 eV (DPP), and for vanadium from 0.18 to 0.27 eV (weak) and 0.22 to 0.48 eV (DPP).

      This gives one predictable local spin per unit, whatever the bridge, on a carbon backbone that is no longer magnetic. The magnetic behaviour is fixed by the metal-oxygen unit, and the charge-carrying behaviour is left to the backbone and the bridge. This is the arrangement the thesis conclusion asked for in a chemical sensor.

    7. 07Density of states with and without oxygen

      The density of states counts how many electron states a chain has at each energy. Here it is plotted against energy relative to the chemical potential (the dotted line at 0), the energy that separates the filled states below from the empty states above. The chemical potential is not the gap itself. It is a single energy, and in these chains it lies inside the gap, between the HOMO below it and the LUMO above it, so the states closest to 0 on either side are the ones that take part in conduction and in any change of spin. Spin-up states are drawn above the axis and spin-down below, with the metal in green, oxygen in black and carbon shaded.

      • Vanadium, weak bridge, bare (top left). Sharp vanadium peaks just below 0, mixed in with carbon states. The metal's d electrons are at the frontier.
      • Vanadium, weak bridge, with oxygen (bottom left). The vanadium peaks move away from 0. One sharp spin-up peak is left 0.75 eV below, with no spin-down partner, which is the single unpaired electron of S = ½. Near 0 there are only carbon states.
      • Iron, fully fused, bare (top right). Iron peaks in both spin channels within about half an electronvolt of 0, mixed in with the carbon.
      • Iron, fully fused, with oxygen (bottom right). Iron and oxygen peaks appear together, 0.5 to 0.75 eV below 0 and about 1.2 eV above, and the carbon states around 0 open into a wider gap (0.34 to 0.64 eV).

      Binding the oxygen pulls the metal's states away from the chemical potential. The spin is then held in the metal-oxygen unit, below the frontier, and the states at the frontier, the ones that would carry a current, are the carbon bands. This means a current along the chain would pass through the carbon backbone while the spin stays on the metal-oxygen unit, so changing what is bound to the metal changes the spin without cutting off the path the current takes.

    8. 08All the design variables together

      The project ran many combinations: five metals, three arm structures, three bridges plus the DPP chain, with and without spin, and with and without oxygen on the metal. This figure gathers the runs that finished.

      The top left panel is the HOMO-LUMO gap of each molecule, by metal and arms. The top right panel is the gap of every chain by bridge, for zinc and for iron and vanadium, bare and with oxygen, with the backbone spin written under each gap. Both use the same log colour scale, dark for small gaps. The bottom left panel is the spin on the ring of each molecule, red aligned with the metal and blue opposite, with the total spin in each cell.       The gaps carry the main result. In single molecules every magnetic metal narrows the gap compared with zinc, to between 0.04 and 0.34 eV, apart from cobalt's plain ring. In chains the bridge sets the gap. The strong bridge gives the smallest gaps (0.05 eV for zinc, 0.13 to 0.21 eV with a metal) and the fully fused bridge the largest (0.34 to 0.78 eV), and oxidising the metal widens the gap in every chain. For conduction alone, the strongly bridged zinc chain is the best candidate, with the smallest gap and a LUMO that runs continuously along the chain.

      In the spin panels, the arms affect the ring's spin in single molecules. Two arms align it with the metal, and four mostly turn it against it. In bare chains (no oxygen on the metal) the bridge affects whether the backbone carries a spin, and only the weak bridge leaves a large one. With oxygen bound the backbone spin is zero in every chain, and the filled points on the right all lie on the zero line. The gap is set by the bridge and the spin by the metal-oxygen unit.

      The most promising combination for further work is the vanadyl (V=O) chain. It has one S = ½ spin per unit on the vanadium, almost nothing on the oxygen or the backbone, and a gap that the bridge moves from 0.21 to 0.60 eV. Of the two oxidised chains it is also the more certain result, since PBE is unreliable for iron spin states. The next calculation would double the unit cell so that neighbouring spins can point opposite ways, and measure the coupling between them through each bridge.

      Much of this project is chemical combinatorics, with metal, arms, bridge and the ligand on the metal each multiplying the number of runs, and each run needing its own convergence tuning. With AI now being applied to DFT, I'd like to take this further with a lightweight, physics-informed model that screens the combinations and sends only the most promising to full DFT. The pieces exist. Neural networks already predict DFT spin-state splittings of transition-metal complexes (Janet & Kulik, 2017) and the gaps of over 12,000 porphyrins (Li et al., 2018), and DeepH learns the DFT Hamiltonian itself, so the orbitals of large structures can be predicted without the self-consistent loop (Li et al., 2022). The catch, which Kulik's group also found, is that the ranking depends on the functional the training data came from, so PBE's weakness for iron spin states would carry over into the model.

    9. 09Work since the capstone

      The capstone was finished in June 2025. A few studies since then are close to it. They are measurements and calculations on real surfaces, where the capstone was a set of PBE calculations on chains in vacuum, without the gold surface, so where they overlap it is in outline only.

      • Vanadyl porphyrins as qubits. Vanadyl (V=O) porphyrins are studied as molecular spin qubits. Privitera et al. (2026) joined two vanadyl porphyrins through a porphyrin bridge, and their spins were independent until light excited the bridge, which coupled them. Vanadyl porphyrins held in a zirconium framework kept their spin coherence (about 1 μs) up to 180 K (Inorg. Chem., 2026). This fits the result here that the V=O spin stays on the vanadium whatever the bridge, though none of this work uses fused chains.
      • Porphyrins on graphene nanoribbons. Xiang et al. (2025) attached metal porphyrins along the edge of a zigzag graphene nanoribbon on gold and measured exchange coupling between distant metal centres, carried by the ribbon's π electrons. In their ribbons the metal's spin stays on the metal. My one Fe=O porphyrin set into a ribbon (the figure) put the spin on the ribbon's carbon instead. It is a different structure and a single PBE run, so I would not read much into the difference.
      • Fused porphyrin chains as spin chains. On gold, fused porphyrin chains of one to seven units each carry an unpaired π electron, and the way the units are fused changes the coupling between neighbouring spins (JACS, 2025). This is close to the bare-chain result here, where the weak bridge leaves unpaired spin on the backbone and the fused bridges mostly do not.

  • Xylella fastidiosa is a bacterium that blocks a tree's water vessels. Since it was found near Gallipoli in 2013 it has killed millions of olive trees in southern Italy, and estimates say it could cost Italy €2 to 5 billion over 50 years if the dead groves are not replanted. A trip to the region, and seeing the impact on the olive groves, got me interested in modelling the disease. This project is a recreation of the agent-based model of Fierro, Liccardo & Porcelli (2019), in which spittlebugs carry the bacterium from tree to tree, rebuilt light enough to run on my old PC and be tuned over multiple runs. The aim was to reconstruct the results of the original paper and, secondly, to bring in percolation theory and look for planting spacings and felling patterns that could break up the spread.

    Via stochastic simulation, it was found that outbreaks are very much all or nothing. 39 of 40 simulated outbreaks infected the whole grove, and below roughly 16% chance of infection per spittlebug visit, outbreaks die out. With trees spaced 8 m apart, and spittlebug numbers per tree kept down by managing the ground cover, the infection no longer spreads through the whole grove. None of the felling patterns tried slowed the spread.

    The difficulty in modelling this disease is that many of the thresholds and interactions have to come from biological studies of how the spittlebugs, the olive trees and the bacterium interact. Meadow spittlebugs sit in the olive trees in spring and early summer and in the meadow grass between them for the rest of the year, and this seasonality is encoded into the model. Infected trees show symptoms about two years after infection, after which spittlebugs begin to avoid them. Current control in Apulia removes the infected tree and the host plants surrounding it, and the ground cover is tilled in spring to kill spittlebug nymphs, which cut their numbers by about 60% in field trials.

    The olive grove modelled in the paper has about a million spittlebugs per hectare, 810,000 on its 90 × 90 m grove, far too many to run multiple times (especially on my old PC). I implemented a lighter version of the model that moves every spittlebug at once instead of one at a time (about 95 times faster) and simulates 810 of them, 0.1% of the paper's number. To check the lighter version, I ran it 200 times and my first, direct implementation of the paper's rules 12 times (that version takes about five minutes a run), and compared the share of the grove infected after 600 days. It was 27% against 29%, with no detectable difference, so moving the spittlebugs at once does not change the results. Simulating only 0.1% of the spittlebugs does change them, making the outbreak slower, and this became the vector-density problem described below.

    An infected spittlebug on a healthy tree infects it with probability p per visit, and the tree shows symptoms after a delay τ drawn from a normal distribution with a mean of 730 days (about two years) and a standard deviation of 100 days, both taken from the paper:

    P(infection on a visit) = p, τ ~ N(730, 100²) days

    As a further attempt to make the runs cheaper, runs at different numbers of spittlebugs were combined with multilevel Monte Carlo. Level 0 simulates 405 spittlebugs and each level doubles the number, so level l simulates 405 × 2ˡ and level 5 simulates 12,960. Q is the share of trees infected after a year:

    E[Q_L] = E[Q_0] + Σₗ E[Q_l − Q_{l−1}]

    The results were mixed, but there were two thresholds of note:

    • Infection per visit. A spittlebug stays on a tree for about 18 days at a time in the paper's model. Via stochastic simulation, it was found that if its chance of passing on the infection during that stay is below roughly 16%, outbreaks die out, and above about 29% they kill the whole grove.
    • Spacing. The spacing of the olive trees can act as a percolation threshold. With the number of spittlebugs per tree held fixed, the infection stops spreading through the whole grove once trees are 6 to 8 m apart. Holding the number per tree fixed means the number per hectare falls as the trees spread out, which on a real farm would mean managing the ground cover where the spittlebugs live. Real groves appear to be planted at roughly this spacing, which gives reason to doubt this result, although slower spread between very widely spaced trees is a fairly obvious result. With spittlebugs per hectare held fixed instead, a wider grove has more grass, and so more spittlebugs, for each tree. Those extra spittlebugs cancel the benefit of the trees being further apart, and 69-85% of trees are infected at every spacing.

    The other results:

    • Outbreaks are all or nothing. 39 of the 40 runs infected the whole grove, and on average half of the grove was infected within about 2.3 years. I expected a heavily infected grove to slow the spread, because spittlebugs avoid trees showing symptoms, and the spread to eventually reach some kind of equilibrium, or culling and certain felling patterns to stop the spread. The results of the simulation found none of these.
    • Felling. Many felling patterns were tried, varying the radius and how quickly infected trees are found. None of this slowed the disease spread.
    • The two-year delay makes intervening difficult. A tree takes about two years to show symptoms, which leaves plenty of time for the bacterium to reach the trees around it before the infected tree can be detected and removed. To stop an outbreak the chance of infection per visit itself has to be low.
    • Multilevel Monte Carlo did not save computing time. Multilevel Monte Carlo estimates the result of a simulation with many spittlebugs from a simulation with few spittlebugs, plus a correction for each doubling of the number of spittlebugs. Each correction is the difference in the share of trees infected after a year between a run with twice as many spittlebugs and a run with half as many, and here the corrections were 0.7, 1.6, 5.5, 13.5 and 26.3 percentage points. A correction is only cheap to estimate if the two runs follow nearly the same outbreak, so each run must contain the same spittlebugs as the run below it, starting in the same places and taking the same path, plus the additional spittlebugs. Both runs also share the first infected spittlebug and each tree's symptom delay. Paired runs correlated at 0.62-0.78, against about zero for pairs of simulations that only share the starting seed. It still took about 2.2 hours of computer time against 1.7 for plain Monte Carlo, because the corrections grew instead of shrinking as the number of spittlebugs rose.
    • The number of spittlebugs simulated changes the speed of the outbreak. The share of trees infected after a year rises from 1% with 405 spittlebugs to 49% with 12,960. Simulating 810 spittlebugs, 0.1% of the paper's number, therefore makes the outbreak slower than the paper's full density would, so the 2.3 years above is slower than in the olive grove modelled in the paper. The checks against the paper were made against my own implementation of the paper's rules at the same reduced density, and the thresholds and felling results are all compared at that one density, so they still compare like with like. Letting each simulated spittlebug stand for a group did not fix this, because the limit is how many trees a swarm reaches, not the chance of infection per visit. The density was not fixed in this project. Setting it to a realistic value, and fitting it to real groves alongside the infection rate, is the main next step.

    We could fit the model to real image sequences, such as the Gallipoli grove that went from full canopies in 2014 to dead trees by 2026. Comparing which trees die and in what order, with a Wasserstein distance (how far one pattern of dead trees has to move to match another), would give the information needed to fit the infection rate and the spittlebug density. With the rates fitted, we could then design felling and ground-cover patterns.

    2. 02Season encoding

      Meadow spittlebugs spend most of the year in the grass and shrubs and move onto the olive trees in spring, when the trees put out new sprouts. The model encodes this with two seasons, using the paper's movement rules:

      • Tender sprouts (days 90 to 185). A spittlebug next to a tree always climbs on (probability 1.0) and rarely leaves (0.005 per attempted step).
      • Hard sprouts (the rest of the year). Spittlebugs are pushed off the trees (1.0) and cannot climb back on (0).
      • Every step. A spittlebug tries to move half the time, 0.5 between grass cells and 0.3 within a tree, with 30 steps a day (the paper uses 48).
      • Each spring (day 120). A new generation of uninfected adults replaces the old one, since nymphs do not carry the bacterium through moulting.
      • Picking up the bacterium. A spittlebug's chance of being infected by a tree rises with how long the tree has been colonised, reaching 1 after about 90 days.

      My first implementation started the grove in the wrong season on day 0, which gave every outbreak a season's head start (27% of the grove infected by day 600 against 20%). Every result here uses the corrected start.

    How the data flows
    1. Grove. A 90 × 90 NumPy array of cell types (shrub 0, tree 1, empty 2), with trees planted as 3 × 3 blocks every 5 cells. Each tree's health, infection day and symptom day are held in dicts keyed by tree position.
    2. Vectors. The spittlebugs are stored as parallel arrays with one entry per insect: x and y positions (int16), and infected and alive flags (bool). The paper's density is sampled down for speed and transmission scaled up to compensate.
    3. Time step. 48 steps per day. Each step, every insect moves to a neighbouring cell with probabilities set by the cell type and the season (tender sprouts before day 185, hard after). An insect on an infected tree may pick up the bacterium; an infected insect on a healthy tree may pass it on.
    4. Record. Each day, the S, I and R tree counts and the infected-insect fraction are appended to history lists. The transmission log adds one row per infection: source tree, target tree, day.
    5. Theory. From the log, transmissions are binned by distance to give T(d), and the front position is fitted against time to give its speed. These feed the percolation calculation and the front-speed formula, which return predicted outbreak size and a predicted timeline.
    6. Output. A three-panel figure (grove map, SIR curves with seasonal phases, insect infection rate), with the theory curves overlaid on the simulated ones.

  • Lattice models (Ising and Potts models) were of big interest to me in college, as was the topic of mathematical ecology. This project is an attempt to represent a coral reef as a Potts model, a grid of cells, each pulled towards the state of its neighbours via nearest-neighbour interaction. A patch with healthy neighbours is more likely to stay healthy, and a patch with bleached neighbours is more likely to bleach. In the global bleaching event of 2023-25, about 84% of the world's reef area had heat stress high enough to bleach coral, and an earlier event at Scott Reef in Western Australia in 2016 gave me bleaching surveys to study. The best-known reef models, such as that of Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007), are mean-field, where every patch of reef feels one uniform field, given by the health of the reef as a whole.

    The aim was to rebuild my undergraduate Potts model in the form of a lattice reef, with patches of healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock and seaweed, and to investigate how the reach of the nearest-neighbour interaction changes the way a reef recovers, compared with the mean-field models in standard use. From physics we know that mean-field models exaggerate tipping points, and I felt that nearest-neighbour coupling would better reflect how real reefs recover. The model was then fitted to the 2016 bleaching event at Scott Reef.

    Via stochastic simulation, it was found that the reef has hysteresis (warmed and then cooled back, it returns along a lower path than it went out on) at every interaction range. Once heat kills enough coral, seaweed grows over the dead coral and blocks new coral from settling, so the coral only returns once the water is cooled well below the temperature where it was lost. In the mean-field reef the gap between the warming and cooling paths is about 60% wider than in the nearest-neighbour reef, where each patch feels only its four nearest neighbours. Cooled back to the starting temperature, the mean-field reef stays degraded at 0.49 coral cover, while the nearest-neighbour reef recovers to about 0.9.

    Real corals differ in how much heat they can take, so each patch of the model reef has its own heat tolerance, drawn at random and smoothed so that neighbouring colonies are alike. The model was fitted to 65 surveys of Scott Reef from April 2016 by simulation-based inference. 8,000 simulated reefs were run through the 2015-16 heatwave and surveyed the same way as the real reef, and a neural network learned which parameters produce which survey. The fit estimates the corals' mean heat tolerance, its spread across the reef, how steeply corals respond once past their tolerance, and the noise. Two different reefs fit the surveys equally well. In the sensitive reef the corals start to feel heat stress at about 4.5 degree heating weeks (a measure of the heat built up over the last 12 weeks), and in the tolerant reef at about 12.7, but they respond more steeply once past it. The surveys were taken at 16.5 degree heating weeks, far beyond both, when both reefs had bleached about 85% of their coral, so they cannot tell the two apart. There seems to be an interplay between the stress threshold and the severity of the reaction past it, which would need further study.

    This model was built for fun, and it could be improved a lot. Access to better coral bleaching data, better data assimilation, a more precise loss function for tuning the parameters and a closer match to the biological processes at play would make for a much more interesting simulation. Real reefs, for example, recover slowly from coral larvae carried in from other reefs, which this model leaves out.

    The reef is a lattice of patches, each healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock or macroalgae (seaweed). Each day every patch may propose a change of state, and what it changes to depends on the water temperature and on its neighbours' states. Healthy neighbours make a patch more likely to be healthy, and bleached neighbours make it more likely to bleach. Coral collapse occurs via two biological mechanisms. Coral under prolonged heat stress dies, and seaweed grows over the dead coral unless grazing fish clear it. The number of fish stays the same as the coral dies, so each patch of seaweed is grazed less often and the seaweed spreads further. In the model, the chance that a patch of seaweed is grazed each day falls as the area without coral grows. This is the feedback Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007) use to make real reefs bistable.

    A proposed change of state is accepted with the Metropolis rule, where ΔE is the change in the patch's energy and β sets how noisy the reef is:

    P(accept) = min(1, exp(−β ΔE))

    A lower energy is a more likely state. For a healthy patch, f_H(x) is the share of its neighbours that are healthy, J is the neighbour coupling (divided by the number of neighbours, so a longer range changes the reach of the interaction and not its total strength), ε_H is the baseline preference for healthy coral, s is the stress sensitivity and D₅₀(x) is the patch's own heat tolerance:

    E_H(x) = −J f_H(x) − ε_H + s · max(DHW(x) − D₅₀(x), 0)

    Heat stress is measured in degree heating weeks (DHW). HS is the sea temperature above the warmest-month mean (MMM), and DHW adds it up over the last 12 weeks, counting only days at least 1 °C above the MMM:

    HS(t) = SST(t) − MMM, DHW(t) = (1/7) Σ_{last 84 days} HS, HS ≥ 1 °C

    The model was fitted to 65 site surveys of Scott Reef in April 2016 by the Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS), using simulation-based inference. Four parameters were fitted: the corals' mean heat tolerance μ_D, its spread across the reef σ_D, the stress sensitivity s (how steeply a coral responds once past its tolerance) and the noise β. The fit was checked on 800 held-out simulated reefs. The main results:

    • Every interaction range has hysteresis. Warmed and then cooled back, the reef comes back along a lower path. Once heat kills enough coral, seaweed grows over the dead coral and blocks new coral from settling, and with fewer fish per patch of seaweed the seaweed stays. In the mean-field reef, where every patch feels the whole reef, the gap between the warming and cooling paths is about 60% wider than in the nearest-neighbour reef, where each patch feels its four nearest neighbours. The mean-field reef also stays degraded, at 0.49 coral cover when cooled back to the baseline, while the nearest-neighbour reef recovers to 0.9, because surviving patches reseed the patches next to them.
    • Two reefs fit the surveys. Via simulation-based inference, it was found that a sensitive reef with mean tolerance 4.5 DHW (37% of the posterior) and a tolerant reef at 12.7 DHW with a larger stress sensitivity s (63%) reproduce the April surveys equally well. The survey was taken at 16.5 DHW, far beyond both tolerances, so it cannot separate how tolerant the corals are from how steeply they respond once past it. There seems to be an interplay between the stress threshold and the severity of the reaction past it, which would need further study.
    • The tipping point of each fitted reef. For 40 parameter sets from the fit, 20 from each reef, the water was held a fixed amount above the warmest-month mean for 120 days, raised 0.1 °C at a time and then lowered again. The sensitive reef tips at +1.1 to 1.2 °C and the tolerant one at +1.4 to 1.7 °C. Unlike the reefs in the hysteresis runs above, neither recovers on cooling in any of the 40 runs. A fitted reef that has died has nothing to regrow from, because the model has no coral larvae arriving from other reefs.

    The other results:

    • Warming speed has little effect. In a grid of heatwaves, how hot the water gets changes how much healthy coral is left afterwards, and how fast it warms changes this very little.
    • No early warning. Rising variance and autocorrelation (Scheffer et al., 2009) and spatial patchiness gave no warning, because under slow warming the coral cover falls gradually rather than suddenly. This was unexpected, as we expected the coral to cluster more before a collapse, and it needs further investigation.

    1. 01Rebuilding the reef model

      We start from my undergraduate Potts model, rewritten in NumPy, which vectorises the update over the whole lattice. The reef is a disc of rock on a 100 × 100 lattice surrounded by open water, with 75% of the rock starting as healthy coral. Each patch is healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock or macroalgae, and each day it may propose a change of state that is accepted with the Metropolis rule. Strictly this is a Potts-inspired stochastic cellular automaton rather than a Potts model in the equilibrium sense, since death and colonisation only go one way.

      In my original code coral could never die. Its death rule needed heat stress above a cap the stress could never reach, so bleached coral always recovered and the reef could never get stuck. Two rules were added, following Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007):

      • Mortality. Bleached coral dies at a daily rate (3%) that rises with how long the heat has lasted.
      • Seaweed and grazing. Macroalgae colonise bare rock, faster next to existing macroalgae, and grazing fish clear them. The same fish are spread over all the non-coral area, so grazing weakens as coral dies.

      The figure is one reef warmed slowly from +2.0 to +4.5 °C above the bleaching baseline, with healthy coral in orange, bleached coral in white, bare rock in grey and macroalgae in green. At +2.0 °C the reef is mostly healthy. By +3.3 and +3.8 °C much of it is bleached, with macroalgae appearing on the dead coral, and by +4.5 °C macroalgae cover most of the reef (the model certainly needs some tuning).     2. 02Interaction range and hysteresis

      The best-known reef models are mean-field, where every patch feels the average of the whole reef. From physics we know that mean-field models exaggerate how sudden and permanent a change of state is, because they leave out the local survivors that can reseed the patches around them. To test this, the reef was run with four interaction ranges, its 4 nearest neighbours, 8, 12, and every patch on the reef (mean field). The neighbour coupling is divided by the number of neighbours, so only the reach of the interaction changes and its total strength stays the same.

      To look for a tipping point we warm the water in steps of 0.25 °C from 0 to +4 °C, holding each step for 60 days, then cool it back down the same way, averaging three runs at each range. If the reef comes back along the same path there is no tipping point. If it comes back along a lower path, the reef has hysteresis, which means it got stuck in the degraded state.

      In the figure the solid lines are the healthy coral cover on warming and the dashed lines on cooling, one colour per interaction range, and the shaded area between them is the hysteresis loop.

      • Every range has hysteresis. At every range the cooling path is below the warming path from +4 °C down to about +0.75 °C. At +1 °C, for example, the reef has about 0.80 cover on warming and 0.52-0.63 on cooling.
      • Short interaction ranges let the reef recover. In the model, bare rock turns back into healthy coral only when at least half of its neighbours are healthy. With 4 to 12 neighbours, a bare patch next to surviving coral meets this condition, so the coral regrows outwards from the survivors. Cooled all the way back to the bleaching baseline (0 °C on the axis), these reefs return to 0.88-0.91 cover, close to where they started.
      • Mean field does not recover. In the mean-field reef every patch sees the average of the whole reef, so the surviving coral does not help the bare patches next to it. Cooled back to the bleaching baseline, the mean-field reef returns to only 0.49 cover, and its loop is about 60% wider than that of the reef with 4 neighbours.

      This means the mean-field model exaggerates collapse, as the physics suggested. With short-range coupling, surviving patches reseed the cells next to them, which softens the collapse.

      The second figure gives the width of each loop, the area between the warming and cooling paths, with the change from the 4-neighbour reef in brackets. Widening the range from 4 to 12 neighbours adds 18%, and the jump to mean field adds 64%.

    3. 03Warming speed and early warnings

      Two further questions were asked. The first is whether a reef warmed quickly fares worse than one warmed slowly to the same peak. The reef was warmed to peaks from +3.5 to +5.0 °C over 5 to 300 days, held for 240 days and cooled for 250.

      In the first figure the colour is the healthy coral cover at the end, the x-axis the days taken to reach the peak and the y-axis the peak. The colour bands run flat across the map, so how hot the water gets changes how much healthy coral is left, and how fast it warms changes this very little. The black lines are the heat added up over the whole run (degree-weeks above 1 °C), and the bands cut across them, so the total heat has little effect either. The temperature the reef is held at has by far the largest effect on the coral left. This result is not very informative, and a more detailed simulation could yield something more interesting.

      The second question is whether a collapse can be seen coming. Three early-warning signals were tracked, the variance and the lag-1 autocorrelation of the coral cover over time (how much one day's value carries into the next), and Moran's I, a measure of how patchy the reef is in space. The reef was warmed slowly from +2.0 to +4.5 °C over 900 days.

      In the second figure the top panel is the coral cover, and the three below are the variance, the autocorrelation and Moran's I, each over a rolling window. The dashed line marks where the cover falls to half its starting value. The variance falls as the cover falls, because under slow warming the cover slides down gradually, and these signals are built to detect an approaching jump. The lag-1 autocorrelation dips to about 0.3 just before the line and rises back to about 0.5 after it, and Moran's I goes up and down with no clear trend. The evidence for an early warning is weak, but a more detailed simulation might give different results.

    4. 04Heat stress and coral tolerance

      The next step was real data, and the aim was to align the simulation with a real bleaching event. Reef data are hard to find, as most surveys cover a few sites at a few dates and say nothing about where on the reef each patch is.

      • Heat stress. The model runs on NOAA Coral Reef Watch's daily degree heating weeks for the Scott-Ashmore station, a standard measure of heat stress on coral. NOAA expects significant bleaching from about 4 DHW and mortality from about 8.
      • Surveys. 65 site surveys by the Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) at Scott Reef on 13-20 April 2016, from the Global Coral Bleaching Database. Each reports bleaching as a category, with 6 sites at 30-60%, 24 at 60-90% and 35 above 90%.
      • Corals with differing heat tolerance. On a single reef, the most heat-tolerant 10% of colonies can take 4-5 DHW more than the least tolerant 10%. Each patch therefore gets its own tolerance D₅₀(x), drawn once from N(μ_D, σ_D²) and smoothed so that neighbouring colonies are alike. In physics terms the uniform field of the Potts model becomes a random field.

      The Scott Reef runs use a 60 × 60 lattice. The top panel of the figure is the heat stress through the 2015-16 summer, with NOAA's bleaching (4) and mortality (8) levels dashed. It passes 4 DHW in early December, reaches about 11 in late January and peaks near 20 in early May. The survey on 16 April is at 16.5 DHW.

      The varied tolerance was not tested on its own. Its mean μ_D and its spread σ_D are two of the four parameters fitted in the next step, so every simulated reef gets a new tolerance field, and the fit estimates how tolerant the corals are and how much they vary. The fit found two reefs that match the survey, a sensitive one and a tolerant one, and the second figure shows one tolerance field for each, at the median fitted parameters. Each patch is coloured by the DHW at which it is as likely to bleach as not.     5. 05Simulation-based inference

      For most models we can write down the probability of the observed data for a given set of parameters (the likelihood) and find the parameters that maximise it. For a stochastic lattice model there is no formula for this probability, and the only way to estimate it is to run the simulation many times. I first tried tuning the model with Optuna, and it was hard to get anything that behaved like a real reef rather than collapsing completely. Instead I used simulation-based inference, which learns the link between the parameters and the data from simulations:

      • Simulate. 8,000 reefs were run through the 2015-16 heat record, with four parameters drawn from wide ranges: mean tolerance μ_D (2-16 DHW), its spread σ_D (0.25-5 DHW), the stress sensitivity s (0.1-1.5) and the noise β (0.05-0.5).
      • Survey. Each simulated reef was surveyed as AIMS surveyed the real one, at 65 random sites on 16 April, binned into the same four bleaching categories.
      • Learn. A mixture density network was trained on these 8,000 simulated surveys. It is a small neural network whose output is a probability distribution rather than a single best guess. For a given survey it gives the weights, centres and widths of several Gaussians, and their sum is the probability of each combination of the four parameters. I wrote it in NumPy and derived the gradients used in training by hand, rather than using a library such as PyTorch that computes them automatically.
      • Check. Before using the real survey, the method was tested on 800 held-out simulated reefs, whose true parameters are known. If the uncertainty it reports is right, the 90% interval should contain the true value for 90% of these reefs, and it did, 89.5-90.4% of the time for each parameter. The third figure repeats this check at every interval level, and all four parameters lie on the dashed diagonal.
      • Fit. Given the real survey, the network returns the posterior, the probability of each combination of parameters. Simulating reefs from it gives on average 1.4, 6.5, 20 and 37 sites in the four categories, against 0, 6, 24 and 35 in the real survey.

      The posterior has two peaks, so two different reefs fit the survey:

      • A sensitive reef. Mean tolerance about 4.5 DHW and a low stress sensitivity (37% of the posterior).
      • A tolerant reef. Mean tolerance about 12.7 DHW and a sharper response to stress (63%).

      In the first figure, the left panel shows every combination of mean tolerance and stress sensitivity, darker where it fits the survey better, and the two peaks are the two reefs. The right panel is the spread of tolerance across the colonies of each reef. Both lie well below the 16.5 DHW at which the survey was taken. A survey taken that far past both thresholds cannot separate how tolerant the corals are from how steeply they respond once past it. The lower panel of the second figure is the bleached share of living coral through the summer in each reef. Both reach about 85% by mid-April, but on 1 February the sensitive reef has 59% bleached and the tolerant one 26%.

    6. 06Scott Reef's tipping point

      The purpose of this step was to estimate the temperature at which the fitted Scott Reef collapses, with error bars from the fit. 40 parameter sets were drawn from the posterior, 20 from each of the two reefs. For each one, the water was held a fixed amount above the warmest-month mean (29.5 °C) for 120 days, raised by 0.1 °C and held again, up to +2.5 °C, then lowered in the same way. The tipping point is where living coral first falls below half its starting value.

      In the figure each line is one parameter set, purple for the sensitive reef and green for the tolerant one. The solid lines are living coral on warming, and the dashed lines, on cooling, all sit at zero.

      • Sensitive reef. Tips at +1.1 to +1.2 °C, about 30.6-30.7 °C.
      • Tolerant reef. Tips at +1.3 to +1.8 °C, most draws between +1.4 and +1.7 °C.
      • Cooling. No run recovers.

      Holding the water at one temperature for 120 days is a much harsher test than a real summer. Degree heating weeks count only days at least 1 °C above the warmest-month mean, so below +1 °C almost no heat stress builds up. Just above +1 °C, 120 days at a fixed temperature builds up more than 12 DHW, well past the sensitive reef's tolerance, so its coral bleaches and dies. The tolerant reef needs a little more heat, so it tips at +1.4 to +1.7 °C.

      In the earlier hysteresis runs some coral survived the warming and regrew outwards on cooling. Here the heat kills nearly all of the coral. In the model, bare rock only turns back into coral when at least half of its neighbours are healthy, so a reef with no coral left has nothing to regrow from. The lattice is also closed, with no coral larvae arriving from outside. Real reefs do recover slowly from larvae carried in from other reefs, and open boundary conditions with a supply of larvae would let the model do the same, so the hysteresis here is an upper bound.

      The second figure plots each draw's tipping point against its mean tolerance. Within the sensitive reef the tipping point barely moves (+1.1 to +1.2 °C for tolerances of 3 to 8 DHW), while within the tolerant reef it rises from +1.3 °C at 8.7 DHW to +1.8 °C at 15.7 DHW.

    7. 07Next steps: more reef data

      The data were the hardest part of this project. I spent a lot of time working on a reef that would collapse totally and not behave like real observed reefs, and this was made harder by the poor data I was using at first. The nearest-neighbour analogy is also only loosely true. Corals do help their neighbours (shade, shelter, larvae settling nearby), but the energies in the model are chosen rather than derived from biological processes, so the results certainly need some work, and the model is only a working proxy.

      • Earlier surveys. Surveys earlier in the year, before most of the coral had bleached, would tell us more about how heat-sensitive a particular reef is than surveys that only see corals that have already bleached. The figure shows each posterior draw's bleached share on 1 February (circles) and 16 April (squares). In April the two fitted reefs overlap, at medians of 82% and 84%, while on 1 February they are at 59% and 26%.
      • Repeated spatial surveys. Surveys of the same sites through a whole season, with their positions on the reef, would let a lattice model be fitted with machine learning rather than tuned by hand.
      • Larval supply. Larvae arriving from neighbouring reefs are the main missing biology. Open boundary conditions with a supply of larvae would let a dead reef recover.

      We could also look at the mathematics of these systems, when a lattice model has a sharp transition and of what order, alongside the applied side.

After reading a few papers on desertification and on spread models, I found it was quite similar to previous projects I had done, and that it suited the GIS skills I have learned over the last year. Gourma's greenness tracks its rain and Fakara's does not as strongly, and across the Sahel, land recovery tracks both rainfall and land use, among other factors, so precipitation cannot be looked at in isolation. With seed rain added (each year a few seeds arrive on the lattice from outside it), the lattice was tested against satellite records from 1982-2021. Its cover correlates with the real greenness about as well as rainfall alone does, and slightly better from year to year.     Read the write-up and code

    My thinking was that a plant seed next to a shrub does better than one on bare ground, because the shrub shades the soil, slows run-off and builds organic matter. Ecologists call this facilitation, hence I thought desertification would fit a lattice model, or cellular automaton, quite nicely. Kéfi et al. (2007) wrote facilitation into a lattice where each cell is vegetated (+), empty but fertile (0) or degraded (−), which lines up well with a Potts model with three states. In Kéfi et al.'s model each cell changes state at the following rates, where q₊ is the share of its four neighbours that are vegetated, ρ₊ the share of the whole lattice, b the environmental quality (here rainfall), δ the share of seeds that travel far, c the competition between plants, m the death rate of plants, d the rate at which bare soil degrades, r the rate at which it recovers on its own and f the neighbour coupling:

    0 → + : [δ ρ₊ + (1 − δ) q₊] (b − c ρ₊), + → 0 : m, 0 → − : d, − → 0 : r + f q₊

    The lattice was compared with the mean-field and pair-approximation theories, then driven with real rain at two Sahel sites with opposite histories (Gourma in Mali re-greened, Fakara in Niger did not), and finally fitted across 223 sites from Senegal to Chad against 40 years of satellite greenness. The main results:

    • The lattice tips and stays tipped. With strong facilitation (f = 0.9), the lattice was dried by slowly lowering the environmental quality b (a stand-in for rainfall, where 1 is the wettest setting) and then re-wetted by raising it again. At b = 0.425 the drying lattice still has 34% vegetation cover, while the re-wetting lattice has only 8%, and it only catches up with the drying lattice near b = 0.55. With weak facilitation (f = 0.2) degraded soil barely heals, and once the vegetation is lost it does not return, even at the wettest setting, b = 1.
    • Patch structure as an early warning. The largest patch holds 90% of the vegetation at b = 0.58 and only 6% at b = 0.50, while the cover is still near 0.5. The collapse comes at b ≈ 0.33, so the percolation threshold (the point where no single patch spans the landscape) is crossed well before the tipping point.
    • Rain alone does not explain the two sites. Gourma's greenness tracks its rain (r = 0.68) and Fakara's hardly does (0.13), so the same lattice on the same kind of rain cannot separate them.

    The other results:

    • Land use needs to be accounted for alongside rainfall. Across the 223 sites, 89% greened by more than their rainfall levels predicted (median +6.3% a decade). Sites with heavier land use greened less beyond this, by 1.1 percentage points a decade per standard deviation of a combined land-use index (90% interval 0.3 to 1.6). The index combines cropland from ESA WorldCover, livestock from FAO's Gridded Livestock of the World and population density from the GPW population grid.
    • With seed rain the lattice is highly correlated with rainfall, and slightly better from year to year. Seed rain means that each year 3% of the empty cells are colonised by seeds from outside the lattice. Tested against 1982-2021 AVHRR greenness (NOAA's satellite record, which covers the droughts), the lattice's cover correlates with each site's year-to-year changes in greenness at a median of 0.26, against 0.19 for smoothed rainfall alone. On the level across sites, the 40-year trend and the recovery from the droughts, the lattice and rainfall do equally well.
    • Recovery from the droughts. Soil quality mattered most, with sandier sites recovering less (3.4 percentage points less per standard deviation of sand by the late 1990s). Heavy land use also weakened the response to returning rain, and clearly so by 2015-21 (1.6 percentage points less recovery per standard deviation of land use).
    • The simulation does not reproduce the clumping of real vegetation. At the same density, real vegetation is about 40% more clumped than the lattice's. Giving the lattice uneven ground, where some cells are always better for plants, narrows the gap, but it makes the lattice's fit to satellite greenness across the Sahel worse.
    • ERA5 is a poor rainfall source for a site study. A known weakness of ERA5 is localised rain in dry climates, where rain falls in brief, local storms. At the two sites it gives about 35% less rain than CHIRPS and IMERG, two satellite rainfall products, and correlates poorly with them from year to year.

    We could follow land-use change over time rather than in snapshots (ESA CCI land cover from 1992), take a more quantitative approach to soil degradation, and run the lattice through a stochastic event set of droughts to test prevention methods such as rotating grazing or protecting the largest patches.

    1. 01Facilitation on a lattice

      The model is Kéfi et al.'s (2007) probabilistic cellular automaton, rebuilt in Numba (a compiler that makes Python loops run about as fast as C). The updates are asynchronous, meaning cells are updated one at a time in random order rather than all at once, and the edges are periodic, meaning the lattice wraps around, so a cell on the right edge is a neighbour of the cell opposite it on the left edge. Each cell is vegetated, empty but fertile, or degraded, and being next to plants raises the rate at which degraded soil recovers (f q₊). This is the same idea as the neighbour coupling in my coral reef model (003). The other rates are Kéfi's defaults, and b is the environmental quality, which stands in for rainfall.

      To look for a tipping point, b (environmental quality) was swept down slowly, then back up, while tracking the vegetation cover. In the figure the solid lines are drying and the dashed lines re-wetting, one colour per strength of facilitation:

      • Strong (f = 0.9). A drying lattice still has 34% cover at b = 0.425 and loses it by 0.325. A re-wetting one has only 8% at the same b and catches up near 0.55, so the way back is longer than the way down.
      • Middle (f = 0.5). The lag is wider.
      • Weak (f = 0.2). Degraded soil barely heals, and once the vegetation is lost it does not return, even at b = 1.
    2. 02Theory against simulation

      Two simpler theories, the mean-field approximation and the pair approximation, were compared with the lattice. Both are sets of differential equations for the share of cells in each state. They were solved by continuation, which follows the steady state of the equations as b changes in small steps, starting each solution from the previous one. The mean field treats every cell as being affected by the state of the whole landscape. The pair approximation also tracks the states of neighbouring pairs of cells, so it keeps the effect of each cell's immediate neighbours, though not of larger patches.

      In the figure the vegetation cover is plotted against b (environmental quality) for strong facilitation (f = 0.9), for the lattice and both theories. The solid lines are drying, as b falls, and the dashed lines re-wetting, as b rises:

      • Mean field. A bare landscape seeded with a few plants never re-greens, at any rainfall (the flat grey dashed line at zero).
      • Pair approximation. A narrow hysteresis window, closed below b = 0.40 and fully open at 0.425.
      • Lattice. The drying lattice collapses near b = 0.3, and the re-wetting lattice (the purple dashed line) recovers, catching up with the drying lattice near b = 0.55. Its hysteresis window is wider than the pair approximation's, but unlike the mean field it does recover.

      This means the mean field, which ignores space, overstates how stuck the landscape gets, and the pair approximation understates it. The mean-field half is the same result as in my coral reef model, where the mean-field reef also exaggerated the tipping point.

    3. 03Patch sizes as an early warning

      A 200 × 200 lattice was dried slowly, and at each step the patches of connected vegetation were labelled. Since the lattice wraps around, a patch that reaches one edge continues on the opposite edge and is counted as one patch.

      In the left panel, green is the vegetation cover and purple the share of all vegetation in the largest patch, with b falling to the right. The largest patch holds 90% of the vegetation at b = 0.58 and only 6% at b = 0.50, while the cover is still near 0.5. The full collapse comes at b ≈ 0.33. This means the percolation threshold, the point where no single patch spans the landscape, is crossed well before the tipping point.

      The right panel is the patch-size distribution, the share of patches at least a given size on log axes, for the lattice at five values of b and for Sentinel-2 vegetation at the two field sites. At high b one patch spans the lattice, seen as the flat tail, and as b falls the large patches vanish first. A satellite counting patch sizes would see the warning, as Kéfi et al. suggested, while one measuring average greenness would not.

    4. 04Two real sites and their rain

      Gourma (Mali) re-greened after the droughts of the 1970s and 80s. Fakara (Niger) did not, under heavy cropping and grazing (Dardel et al., 2014). The two had similar rainfall histories, so if rain sets the vegetation the model should follow both. The first figure is Sentinel-2 NDVI over 2 × 2 km at each site beside the model lattice, which shows the same kind of patchy structure.

      The rain turned out to be the weak point. ERA5 spreads rain over boxes about 30 km across, while Sahel rain comes in brief, local storms. It was compared with two satellite products over the same half-degree box, CHIRPS (satellite blended with rain gauges, daily from 1981) and IMERG (satellite only, monthly from 2000). In the second figure each site's rainy-season total from the three products is plotted by year:

      • ERA5. About 35% less rain (204 mm a season at Gourma against 321 for CHIRPS and 373 for IMERG), and it puts Gourma's driest season in 2021 rather than the 1980s.
      • Agreement. CHIRPS and IMERG correlate at 0.58 (Gourma) and 0.74 (Fakara), while ERA5 and IMERG barely do (0.29 and 0.47).

    5. 05The lattice on real rain

      The lattice was driven with each site's rainy-season rain from 1950 to 2025, scaled so that the wet 1950s and 60s are at b = 0.75, and compared with MODIS greenness (NDVI) from 2000. The first figure shows the rain, the lattice cover and MODIS greenness for each site.

    6. 06What goes with greening beyond rain

      The question then moved from two sites to the whole Sahel. A grid every 0.5° of latitude and 1° of longitude from Senegal to Chad, between 13° and 17° N, gives 297 boxes of about 22 km, 223 of them vegetated enough to measure. For each, Earth Engine gives MODIS greenness and CHIRPS rain each year 2001-2024, cropland (ESA WorldCover), livestock (FAO GLW4), population (GPW) and topsoil sand and organic carbon.

      At each site greenness was regressed on that year's rain, and the trend in what rain does not explain (a Theil-Sen slope) is the land gain or loss beyond the weather. This is RESTREND (Evans & Geerken, 2004). The site trends were then regressed on the covariates, with 90% intervals from a block bootstrap over 4° bands of longitude, since neighbouring sites share weather and land use.

      In the figure the map is the rain-adjusted trend at every site, blue for greening, with the two field sites starred. The lower panels are the effect of each covariate with its interval, and the trend against cropland and livestock with the median of each eighth.

      With Kéfi's default rates the lattice tracked MODIS poorly, so it was fitted at the two sites. The rain scale and a degradation rate, constant to 1985 then changing linearly to 2025, were found by grid search on 2001-2015 and tested on 2016-2025 against rain alone.

      In the figure the top panels are MODIS greenness, the fitted lattice and rain alone at each site, test years in grey, and the lower panels the fit over the grid of rates.

      • Fakara. Nothing fits. Rain alone explains 1% of its greenness, and any fit that leans on the lattice runs away on the test years.

      At one 5 km site and 15 fitting years, peak greenness is the grass's response to rain, and the slow cover the lattice models cannot be picked out of it.

    8. 08One set of rates for the Sahel

      The target then changed to dry-season greenness (March-May MODIS NDVI), when the grass has died back and what is left is mostly shrubs and trees.     9. 09Seed rain and a test through the droughts

It is small enough to put a PDE mesh and machine learning on directly, and it has two rich sources of data, ERA5-Land, with boxes about 9 km across, and a network of weather stations.

    The aim was a calibrated 1 km model of daily maximum temperature for the Po Valley that matches every station reading exactly. A heat-budget equation anchored to the stations was compared with a U-Net, a physics-informed U-Net, ERA5-Land and regression with kriged residuals, the standard statistical method. It was built and checked on simulated fields with a known truth, then run on real data from ERA5-Land, MODIS and 38 stations for the summers of 2021-2023.

    The denser station networks of the regional environmental agencies (ARPA), with about ten times as many stations, would give enough data to separate the best models.

    Read the write-up and code

    ERA5-Land describes the Po Valley in boxes about 9 km across. Gridded station products such as E-OBS fill the map by interpolating stations, and in doing so they no longer return the station readings themselves. This project builds daily maximum temperature on a 1 km grid for June to August 2021-2023 under two constraints. Between stations the surface obeys a reduced heat-budget equation, and at stations it equals the observation exactly. The method was built and checked first on simulated fields with a known truth, then run on real data: ERA5-Land, MODIS land surface temperature, terrain and land cover from Earth Engine, and 38 Meteostat stations.

    Temperature is reduced to sea level, θ = T + Γz with Γ = 6.5 K/km. Each day the model solves a steady heat budget, where u is the ERA5-Land wind, κ a mixing coefficient, θ_s the MODIS land surface temperature, τ_s(x) how quickly the air takes up the ground's temperature (set by the land-cover fractions f_c), θ_E the ERA5-Land temperature with relaxation time τ_a, and q(x) the heating the equation leaves out:

    u · ∇θ − κ ∇²θ + (θ − θ_s) / τ_s(x) + (θ − θ_E) / τ_a = q(x), 1 / τ_s(x) = Σ_c f_c(x) / τ_c

    Discretised, this is a sparse system Aθ = b + q. With H picking out the station cells and y the readings, the anchored surface uses the smallest and smoothest q that makes the surface equal every station, where G = HA⁻¹ needs one extra (adjoint) solve per station and W penalises size and roughness:

    q = q̂ + W⁻¹Gᵀ (G W⁻¹ Gᵀ)⁻¹ (y − Hθ₀), θ₀ = A⁻¹(b + q̂)

    The physics-informed network is a U-Net trained on the stations S, with the same heat budget as a penalty at every land cell Ω, weighted by λ:

    loss = (1/|S|) Σ_S (θ̂_s − y_s)² + pinball(5%, 95%) + λ (1/|Ω|) Σ_Ω (Aθ̂ − b)²

    Every model was scored at stations held out in 50 km blocks in 2023, after fitting on 2021-2022. The main results on the real data:

    • Every regional model beats ERA5-Land. RMSE falls from 1.89 K for lapse-corrected ERA5-Land to 1.60-1.62 K for regression-kriging, the physics model and the physics-informed network, about a sixth of ERA5-Land's error. These three are within 0.05 K of each other, and 38 stations cannot separate them.
    • The heat budget helps the network. Adding it to the loss takes the U-Net from 1.71 K to 1.60 K (λ = 0.3). With the heaviest weight (λ = 3) and anchored to the stations, it is the best model on the hottest 5% of days, at 1.60 K against 2.05 K for ERA5-Land and 1.87 K for kriging.
    • The anchoring holds. On all 276 days the surface returns every station reading to within 10⁻¹² K.

    The other results:

    • The physics reaches only a few kilometres. The fitted relaxation time is about 1.2 hours, so a station's influence on the map runs about 7 km downwind on a median day, 4 km on the calmest and 14 km on the windiest. Beyond that the statistical prior has to carry the information.
    • The XGBoost prior does not carry over. On the simulated data, XGBoost learning the missing heating from land cover and terrain gave the best model (0.79 K). On the real data it made the anchored model worse (1.61 to 1.75 K).
    • The U-Net emulator smooths the extremes. Trained to reproduce the physics surface from coarse maps, it comes close overall (1.65 K) but scores 1.85 K on the hottest days.
    • Above 800 m no model improves on lapse-corrected ERA5-Land (1.74 K), as a fixed lapse rate is used everywhere.

    We could separate the best models with the regional ARPA station networks, which have about ten times as many stations. A lapse rate fitted each day and a second-order advection scheme, so that the fitted mixing coefficient can be read physically, would improve the physics itself.

    1. 01Testing the solver on a planted truth

      Before any real data, the solver was tested on a truth built from the equation itself. Daily fields were generated from the heat budget with known parameters, eight in all (the mixing coefficient κ, the atmospheric relaxation time τ_a and one relaxation time τ_c per land-cover class), and the fit was asked to recover them from 20 days of station readings. This was done twice:

      • Physics complete (q = 0). The equation used to fit is the one that made the data, and every parameter comes back exactly.
      • Physics missing (q unknown). A hidden heating term is added to the data. The relaxation times come back to within about 25%, while κ comes back at about 0.4 of its true value. The anchoring still returns every station to within 3 × 10⁻¹⁴ K, and the planted heating is recovered with a correlation of 0.97 where stations can see it.

      The parameters are fitted with adjoint gradients, where one extra solve per day gives the derivative of the error with respect to all eight at once, checked against finite differences. In the figure each row is one parameter, the x-axis is the fitted value over the true one on a log scale, and the shaded band is a factor of two either side. The dot size is the share of the stations' surroundings in that land-cover class, so a small dot is a parameter the stations see little of.

      • Boundary and background. ERA5-Land daily maximum temperature, about 9 km, lapse-corrected to sea level, sets θ on the boundary and the background θ_E.

      • Coupling. ESA WorldCover 2021 land-cover fractions in six classes set τ_s(x). On the real data the fitted relaxation time is about 1.4 hours over tree cover, against 28 hours for built-up land and 37 hours for cropland.
      • Stations. 38 Meteostat stations with a 2021-2023 record, 276 summer days.

    3. 03Anchoring the surface to every station

      On its own the heat budget scores 1.62 K RMSE at held-out stations on the real data. The anchoring adds the smallest and smoothest missing heating q that makes the surface pass through every reading. Each station's footprint, the row of G = HA⁻¹, comes from one adjoint solve, so the correction for all stations follows in closed form. The smoothness length ℓ_q = 60 km was chosen by searching 10, 25 and 60 km on held-out stations.

      The first figure shows sea-level temperature as a surface on the hottest July 2022 day, with each station as a pin whose head is the reading. On the left, physics only, many pins stand above or below the surface. On the right, anchored, the surface passes through every pin. On all 276 days the largest miss at any station is 10⁻¹² K.

      The second figure is the footprint of six stations on the calmest and windiest days, darker where a reading changes the map more, with the ERA5-Land wind as arrows. The fitted relaxation time is short (about 1.2 hours), so the footprints are small:

      • Calmest day (3 km/h). Each footprint is a near-round patch, about 4 km in e-folding length.
      • Windiest day (12 km/h). The footprints stretch downwind, about 14 km.
      • Mixing. The fitted κ is about forty times smaller than the numerical diffusion of the upwind scheme, so it cannot be read physically, and the wind term is worth 0.045 K.

      This means the physics carries a station's reading only a few kilometres, and further than that the map depends on the prior for q.

    4. 04Scoring at held-out stations

    5. 05The missing heating and the XGBoost prior

      The heating q that the anchoring adds is the part of the temperature the reduced heat budget cannot explain, such as sea breezes, inversions and boundary-layer depth. XGBoost (a gradient-boosted tree model) was trained, fold by fold, to predict q from land cover, elevation, slope, distance to the coast, the surface-temperature excess, wind and day of year, and its prediction q̂ is handed back to the anchoring as the prior.

      In the figure the left map is the mean q over all days on the real data, red where the equation runs too cold and heat had to be added, blue where it runs too warm, with the stations as dots. The right map is the land cover. The corrections are concentrated around the stations, which matches the short footprints of the previous step.

      • Simulated data. The XGBoost prior gave the best model, 0.79 K against 0.83 K for plain anchoring.
      • Real data. It made the anchored model worse, 1.75 K against 1.61 K. Elevation (0.18), distance to the coast (0.15) and slope (0.11) were its most used features, and with 38 stations it has little real q to learn from. On the simulated check (second figure) q follows the land-cover zones instead, negative along the coasts and lakes.
    6. 06A U-Net emulator of the physics

      The usual way to put a neural network into downscaling is to train it on a dense target. A U-Net (Ronneberger, Fischer & Brox, 2015), the standard image-to-image network in statistical downscaling (Baño-Medina, Manzanas & Gutiérrez, 2020), takes 14 coarse and static maps (ERA5-Land, the surface-temperature excess, wind, terrain and six land-cover fractions) and returns a 1 km field with a 5% and a 95% quantile. It has 120,000 parameters and is written in JAX.

      Its target is the anchored physics surface, built for each fold without that fold's stations, so it learns to reproduce the physics model from coarse inputs alone. This is an emulator, not a fair contest with the physics, and it shows how much of the anchored surface can be predicted from the coarse fields.

      • Overall. 1.65 K on the real data against 1.62 K for physics only (1.03 K for both on the simulated check).
      • Hottest 5% of days. 1.85 K against 1.67 K. An emulator smooths its target, and the extremes are what get smoothed away.
      • Intervals. Its 90% interval held 63% of real 2023 readings. Conformalised quantile regression (Romano, Patterson & Candès, 2019) widens it by the 90th percentile of the 2022 misses, which on the real data overshoots to 95% with a mean width of 6.6 K (86% and 3.0 K on the simulated check).

      The figure is from the simulated check, on the hottest 2023 day. On the left is the anchored physics surface and on the right the U-Net's, with the eight stations of the held-out fold as pins. The U-Net surface is smoother than the physics surface, with less of the cell-to-cell detail. The second figure shows RMSE over all days (blue) and on the hottest 5% (orange) for the two physics models and the emulator. On the real data the emulator's error grows the most on the hottest days.

    7. 07The heat budget in the network's loss

      A fair comparison trains the network on the thermometers themselves. The same U-Net was trained on the stations with the heat-budget residual Aθ̂ − b as a penalty at every land cell, using the same stencil and fitted parameters as the physics model. This is the soft-constraint form of physics-informed learning (Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis, 2019). Strictly it is a physics-regularised U-Net, since the residual is the discretised one rather than the derivative of a continuous network. The physics term needs no labels, so it reaches every cell between the stations, where a data-only network has nothing to learn from. λ was reported at four settings rather than tuned, each trained per fold for 1,200 steps.

      In the first figure, the left panel is RMSE against λ: at the training stations in a validation month (grey, dashed), at held-out stations in 2023 (blue) and at held-out stations more than 60 km from an anchor (green). The right panel is RMSE for each model in four groups of held-out stations, one marker per model.

      • Training stations. The error rises with λ, from 1.55 K to 1.74 K, because the physics stops the network fitting those thermometers as closely.
      • Held-out stations. It falls from 1.71 K (data only) to 1.60 K at λ = 0.3, and 1.63 K at λ = 3.
      • Hottest 5% of days. 1.88 K data only, 1.67 K at λ = 3, and 1.60 K at λ = 3 anchored to the stations, the best of any model.
      • Far from anchors. More than 60 km from a fitted station the data-only network scores best (1.42 K against 1.49-1.52 K), the one group where the physics term did not help.

      The second figure is the hottest 2023 day (23 August) on the real data, from the data-only network (left) and with the heat budget at λ = 3 (right), with the eight held-out stations of fold 0 as pins. The data-only map is noisy from cell to cell, and the physics term gives a smooth field that follows the valley. The third figure is the same comparison on the simulated check, where the gain on the hottest days was larger, from 1.12 K to 0.85 K.

    8. 08Next steps: denser station data

      With 38 stations the best models are within 0.05 K of each other and cannot be separated, and the station network is uneven. The map shows the stations coloured by elevation, and the histogram compares the elevations of the stations with those of the land. 61% of stations are below 200 m, against 41% of the land.

      • More stations. The regional ARPA networks (Piemonte, Lombardia, Veneto, Emilia-Romagna) have about ten times as many stations, which would both separate the best models and give the XGBoost prior enough missing heating to learn from.
      • A lapse rate fitted each day. Above 800 m no model beats ERA5-Land with a fixed 6.5 K/km, and a daily lapse rate from the mountain stations would address this.
      • A second-order advection scheme. The first-order upwind scheme adds about 1,000 m²/s of numerical diffusion, more than the fitted κ, so a higher-order scheme would let the mixing be read physically.

      We could then benchmark the 1 km map against ERA5 alone on the heatwave days that matter for industry in the valley.

    How the data flows

    The code was checked end to end on simulated daily fields of the same shape, then run on the real products fetched by download_data.py.

    1. Input. ERA5-Land daily maximum temperature, skin temperature and afternoon wind at 0.1°. MODIS Aqua daytime land surface temperature at 1 km. Elevation and six land-cover fractions per 1 km cell. Daily maxima at 38 stations.
    2. Daily fields. Everything is reduced to sea level with a 6.5 K/km lapse rate. Cloud gaps in the MODIS field are filled with ERA5-Land skin temperature plus each cell's mean clear-sky offset for the month.
    3. Physics. A steady advection-diffusion-relaxation equation is assembled as a sparse system and factorised once per day. Its relaxation times, one per land-cover class, are fitted with adjoint gradients.
    4. Anchor and learn. One adjoint solve per station gives its footprint. The smallest, smoothest forcing q that makes the surface equal every station is found in closed form. XGBoost learns q from land cover and terrain and supplies it as a prior.
    5. Learn. A U-Net emulates the anchored surface with conformal intervals. A physics-informed U-Net is trained on station readings with the heat-budget residual in its loss.
    6. Output. Daily maximum temperature at 1 km that returns every station reading exactly, a map of the heating the equation lacks, and a skill table from 50 km blocks held out in a year never used for fitting.

  • A fleet of solar plants carries correlated risk. If each site's P95, its output in a bad year, is worked out on its own, the fleet's bad year is underpriced, because the same clouds cover many sites at once. This project builds the framework for measuring that shared risk, the step that has to come before simulated weather or stochastic event sets can price it.

    The aim was to measure how far the weather stays correlated across 25 Spanish solar regions, what that does to the fleet's output and revenue at OMIE prices, and how ECMWF's physics (IFS) and AI (AIFS) forecast models handle it, using ERA5 solar radiation built into an Anemoi dataset.

    It was found that the correlation falls to 1/e over 350 to 670 km depending on the season, so the 25 sites behave like only 3-4 independent ones. Priced at OMIE's 2023 prices, the fleet's worst 5% of days produced 16% less than independent sites would and earned about €350 against €2,500. Furthermore, it was found over 30 years of ERA5 that the fleet's 1-in-20 bad month is 10.5% below normal, four times deeper than independent sites give, which a copula event set reproduces, while forecast errors are far more local than the weather and cancel across the fleet.

    This points towards pricing a solar fleet's P95 on the shared weather and the shared market, and towards repeating the analysis on CERRA at 5.5 km.

    A solar fleet spread across a country should smooth itself out, since when it is cloudy in Seville it may be sunny in Zaragoza. How much smoothing there is depends on the distance over which the weather stays correlated. Generation at 25 Spanish solar regions is proxied by ERA5 surface solar radiation, built into an Anemoi dataset (the layout ECMWF's AI forecast models read), and expressed as the clear-sky index, the share of the possible sunlight that gets through the cloud.

    The correlation between two sites falls with their distance d, fitted as a stretched exponential with reach L, the distance at which it falls to 1/e:

    ρ(d) = exp[ −(d / L)^α ]

    How many independent sites the fleet behaves like is the participation ratio of the correlation matrix's eigenvalues λᵢ:

    N_eff = (Σᵢ λᵢ)² / Σᵢ λᵢ²

    The forecasts of ECMWF's two models, IFS (physics) and AIFS (AI, trained on ERA5), are scored against climatology:

    skill = 1 − MSE(forecast) / MSE(climatology)

    The main results:

    • The fleet behaves like 3-4 independent sites. The correlation falls to 1/e over about 350 km in winter, 440 km in spring and summer and 670 km in autumn, so the 25 sites diversify like only 3-4 independent ones on the hourly clear-sky index.
    • Shared weather and shared prices deepen the bad days. Sold at OMIE's 2023 day-ahead prices, the fleet's worst 5% of days produced 16% less than independent sites would, and earned about €350 against €2,500, with 36 days of midday prices below €10/MWh.
    • The 1-in-20 bad month is four times deeper than independent sites give. 10.5% below normal over 30 years of ERA5 (1995-2024), against 2.5% with the sites independent, which a Gaussian copula event set reproduces.

    The other results:

    • A first artefact. On real data every site first seemed almost perfectly correlated, because of a daily shape left by the clear-sky model that every site shares. It was removed before correlating.
    • Spreading the sites helps everyday correlation but not the worst days. Six sites in other weather regimes lift the fleet from 2.1 to 3.1 effective sites on daily output, but the worst 5% of days stay at about 37% of an average day, as they are winter fronts over the whole peninsula.
    • Forecast errors are local. Day-3 errors decorrelate over 167-233 km against about 506 km for the weather, so pooling the fleet cancels forecast error much better than it diversifies the weather.
    • AIFS against IFS. At a single site AIFS scores higher from day 2 (0.73 against 0.57 at day 4), and for the fleet IFS leads on days 1-2 (0.93 against 0.89 on day 1).

    We could repeat the analysis on CERRA at 5.5 km, since ERA5's 31 km grid smooths small clouds and these lengths are upper bounds, and score the forecasts in money with prices and cannibalisation.

    6. 06A first stochastic event set

    7. 07Thirty years and the copula's tails

    rₜ = a + b Δln(BRL/USD)ₜ + εₜ

    Extremes arriving together are modelled with a Gumbel copula, a joint distribution of the ranks u and v of two series with a parameter θ ≥ 1, whose upper-tail coefficient λ_U is the chance that one series is in its extreme top when the other is:

    C(u, v) = exp( −[ (−ln u)^θ + (−ln v)^θ ]^(1/θ) ), λ_U = 2 − 2^(1/θ)

    The forward curve follows Schwartz's (1997) two-factor model, in which X is the log spot price, δ the convenience yield (the benefit of holding physical coffee rather than a contract), κ the speed at which δ returns to its long-run level α, and Wₜ a weather term that pushes δ with strength β:

    dX = (μ − δ − ½σ₁²) dt + σ₁ dz₁, dδ = [ κ(α − δ) + β Wₜ ] dt + σ₂ dz₂

    The seasonal forecast is scored against climatology, a normal season for the time of year:

    skill = 1 − MSE(forecast) / MSE(climatology)

    A Sentinel-2 pixel is counted as hot when its shortwave infrared reflectance ρ climbs steeply towards band 12 (2.2 μm), which only happens above a few hundred degrees (Murphy et al. 2016):

    ρ₁₂ / ρ₁₁ ≥ 1.4, ρ₁₂ / ρ₈ₐ ≥ 1.4, ρ₁₂ ≥ 0.15

    The ship signal at each port is its last four weeks of dry-bulk exports X as a log ratio to the same four weeks a year earlier, and it is scored out of sample against the expanding-window mean of the price change y (Campbell & Thompson 2008):

    sₜ = ln( X₄w,ₜ / X₄w,ₜ₋₅₂w ), R²_oos = 1 − Σ(yₜ − ŷₜ)² / Σ(yₜ − ȳₜ)²

    Ships in Sentinel-1 radar are found with a CFAR (constant false alarm rate) detector, which flags a pixel x when it stands well above the mean μ and standard deviation σ of a ring of sea 250-500 m around it:

    x > μ_ring + 5 σ_ring

    Gas plants set the Italian power price in most hours, so the price mostly follows Dutch TTF gas. Everything here works on power relative to gas, log(power / gas), so that what is left can be tied to the weather. The data are 2018-2026 day-ahead prices for the Italy North zone (Energy-Charts, from ENTSO-E), Italian load, wind and solar, an eight-city ERA5 temperature and TTF gas. The method was first built and checked on simulated data with planted relationships, then run on the real series in three stages: what drives the price, what drives its volatility, and how much a weather forecast could add.

    The volatility model is GARCH-MIDAS (Engle, Ghysels & Sohn, 2013). Daily returns r_t have a short-run GARCH part g_t, which captures clustering, and a slow monthly level τ_m set by the extreme-day counts X of the previous months, with lag weights φ_k(w) and θ the size of the weather effect:

    r_t = μ + √(τ_m g_t) ε_t, g_t = (1 − α − β) + α (r_{t−1} − μ)² / τ_m + β g_{t−1}

    log τ_m = m + θ Σ_k φ_k(w) X_{m−k}

    A month's extreme-day count is the number of days with an Italy-wide mean temperature above 28 °C or below 3 °C. The main results:

    • Extreme weather calms the volatility. Via maximum likelihood on the real series, it was found that the extreme-day count is significant (likelihood ratio 27.6, p ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻⁷), but with a negative sign (θ = −0.15). This is the opposite of Guerzoni, Riso & Zoia (2026), who found extreme weather the main driver of Italian price volatility. A bootstrap of 499 series with no weather effect never reached 27.6 (p ≈ 0.002).
    • A forecast of the extremes adds almost nothing. Fitted on 2018-2022 and scored from 2023, knowing each month's count in advance lowers the volatility forecast loss by 0.7% against plain GARCH, and last month's count by 0.9%.
    • Day to day the weather is clear. Demand, renewables, heat and weekends, learned on 2018-2022, explain 47% of the daily swings of power relative to gas in 2023-2026, out of sample. In summer 2026 power ran about 11% higher relative to gas on days above 28 °C.

    The other results:

    • The price is U-shaped in temperature. XGBoost on power relative to gas beats a straight line on unseen years (RMSE 0.174 against 0.255), and SHAP shows power dearer in cold weather and above about 27 °C, cheapest around 18-25 °C.
    • The gas link changes over time. Weekly power and gas moved almost one for one through the 2022 gas crisis (rolling correlation near 0.9) and hardly at all late in the 2026 heatwave.
    • A real seasonal forecast adds nothing. ECMWF's SEAS5 three-month temperature forecast, fed through the 2018-2022 relationship, scored worse than climatology on the level (skill −3.9) and no better on volatility (−0.02).

    This is a maths note (16 pages, LaTeX) on how far one failure spreads through a connected network. The tools come from ecology, following May, Levin and Sugihara (2008), who proposed using them for banking. These are May's stability bound, percolation thresholds and disassortative wiring. Each is derived, checked by simulation on networks of 10,000 nodes, and then applied to two systems, German industry when the Rhine runs dry and a structured credit portfolio.

    May's bound says a large random system of N parts, each pair linked with probability C (the connectance) and with link strengths of standard deviation σ, is stable only while

    σ√(NC) < 1

    For a network in which a failure passes along each link with probability T (the transmissibility), the failure spreads to a finite share of the whole only above a threshold set by the mean degree ⟨k⟩ and the mean squared degree ⟨k²⟩, or with degree correlations by the leading eigenvalue Λ of the matrix of (k′ − 1) times the chance P(k′|k) that a node of degree k links to one of degree k′:

    T_c = ⟨k⟩ / (⟨k²⟩ − ⟨k⟩), T_c = 1 / Λ[ (k′ − 1) P(k′|k) ]

    When each firm also fails on its own with probability p, the failed fraction comes from the generating functions G₀ and G₁ of the degree and excess-degree distributions, where v is the chance that a link followed outward does not lead to failure:

    v = 1 − T + T(1 − p) G₁(v), failed fraction = 1 − (1 − p) G₀(v)

    For credit, the transmissibility moves with a common economic factor Z (large Z is a bad year), with T̄ its average and β the factor loading:

    T(Z) = Φ( [Φ⁻¹(T̄) + √β Z] / √(1 − β) )

    The main results:

    • One aggregate number does not pin down the network. A Rhine-basin network of 4,000 firms calibrated so that a low-water month costs about 1% of German industrial output is reproduced by economies anywhere from 6% to 89% of the way to their percolation threshold.
    • Past the threshold the average loss moves. If road and rail cannot replace the barges (T = 1.25 T_c), the same low water costs about 38% of a month's output, and the cascade reaches Stuttgart, Munich and Berlin.
    • The three models differ in the tail. With the same failure chance for each firm, the worst 1% of months loses 1.8% of output with independent failures, 2.3% through the network and 7.4% with a one-factor correlation model.

    The other results:

    • May's bound. Simulated community matrices fill the predicted disc, and the probability of stability drops from one to zero at σ√(NC) = 1, more sharply as N grows.
    • Disassortative wiring. Linking hubs to small nodes doubles the threshold for the same degree sequence (T_c from 0.072 to 0.149), but above T ≈ 0.28 the same wiring fails most completely.
    • Duration. Over a four-month low-water episode each model loses about 6% of one month's output, and the worst 1% of episodes loses 8.4% (independent), 10.2% (network) and 26% (correlated).
    • Structured credit. At the same 1.9% mean loss as a Vasicek model with ρ = 0.2, the 15-30% tranche carries 46 times the expected loss and the equity tranche about 40% less.

    1. 01May's stability bound

      May (1972) showed that a large random ecosystem is stable only while σ√(NC) < 1. The community matrix is A = −I + B, where each part regulates itself at unit rate and each ordered pair interacts with probability C, with a strength drawn from a normal distribution of standard deviation σ. By the circular law (Tao & Vu 2010) the eigenvalues of B fill a disc of radius σ√(NC), so once the disc crosses zero an eigenvalue of A has a positive real part and the system tips.

      In the figure, panels (a) and (b) are the eigenvalues of one simulated matrix below and above the bound, with the real part on the x-axis, the imaginary part on the y-axis and the predicted disc drawn as a circle. Panel (c) is the share of simulated matrices that are stable against σ√(NC), one line for each size N. - the eigenvalues fill the predicted disc - the probability of stability drops from one to zero at σ√(NC) = 1 - the drop is sharper for larger N

    2. 02Percolation thresholds

      Percolation is the network version of the same question. Each link passes a failure on with probability T, and above the threshold T_c the failure reaches a finite share of the whole network rather than dying out. For a random network T_c = ⟨k⟩ / (⟨k²⟩ − ⟨k⟩), the inverse of a leading eigenvalue like May's bound.     The P wave is the first wave to arrive from an earthquake. The time it arrives at each station is used to locate the quake, and whether the ground first moves up or down (the first motion) shows how the fault slipped. Earthquake catalogues are built on these two numbers, so they are what any denoising has to preserve. The data are 3,000 recordings from the noisiest fifth of a labelled Southern California Seismic Network dataset (Ross, Meier & Hauksson, 2018), small (M ≤ 3.5) local (≤ 50 km) earthquakes with an analyst's arrival time and first motion on each, 6 s at 100 Hz with a median signal-to-noise ratio of 2.7.

    Arrivals are picked with an STA/LTA trigger, the standard picker in seismology. It compares the energy in a short window (0.1 s) with a long one (1 s) and picks the first sample where the ratio passes 6:

    R(t) = STA(t) / LTA(t) = [ (1/n_s) Σ_{last 0.1 s} x² ] / [ (1/n_l) Σ_{last 1 s} x² ], pick = first t with R(t) > 6

    The random-matrix denoiser keeps the principal components of the traces whose eigenvalues lie above the Marchenko-Pastur edge for n samples per trace and m traces:

    λ₊ = (1 + √(n / m))²

    For an array, the minimum-variance (Capon) weights give the least noise for a given noise covariance Σ while passing the aligned wave a at unit gain:

    w = Σ⁻¹ a / (aᵀ Σ⁻¹ a)

    The main results:

    • Moving the arrival changes the ranking. Every trace in the dataset has its arrival at the same sample. Scored as they come, the random-matrix denoiser picked 98% of arrivals. With each arrival moved at random it fell to 60%, against 56% with no denoising.
    • Only a bandpass filter helps without harm. It raised the arrivals picked from 56% to 63% and kept the first motion on 91% of traces, the same as no denoising.
    • A cleaner-looking waveform can be worse for picking. Wavelet thresholding added about 3 dB of signal-to-noise and picked the most arrivals (68%), but the first motion matched the analyst on 84% of traces against 91%, a flip on about one trace in fourteen.

    The other results, on a synthetic array where the truth is known:

    • Shared noise holds back averaging. With noise from a road shared between neighbouring sensors, a plain average of 32 sensors reduced the noise by 10.9 dB against 15.1 dB for independent noise. The minimum-variance stack, with a cleaned covariance, reached 13.9 dB.
    • The covariance has to be cleaned. Raw minimum-variance weights needed about 0.8 s of noise to beat the average. Cleaned with nonlinear shrinkage (corrlib), they beat it from 0.2 s.

    Read the write-up and code

    corrlib is a small Python library for measuring, cleaning and stripping down correlation matrices, which this portfolio's projects import instead of rewriting. It has five modules: correlation measures, estimators for messy data (prices at irregular times, samples that are not independent), factor removal, random-matrix cleaning and array stacking. A Correlator object chains them in one line. Each piece is tested against a known answer or an established library, and shown on ten years of daily closes for 39 US stocks in eight sectors.

    With N variables and T observations, a correlation matrix of pure noise has its eigenvalues inside the Marchenko-Pastur band, where q = N/T and σ² is the variance of the noise. Eigenvalues outside the band are structure, and the cleaners shrink the ones inside it:

    λ± = σ² (1 ± √q)²

    Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage pulls the sample matrix S towards a scaled identity by an intensity δ estimated from the data, and nonlinear shrinkage (Ledoit & Wolf, 2020) gives each eigenvalue its own amount. The effective rank, the number of independent bets the variables offer, is computed from the eigenvalues λᵢ:

    Σ̂ = (1 − δ) S + δ μ I, effective rank = (Σᵢ λᵢ)² / Σᵢ λᵢ²

    The minimum-variance weights for a portfolio and the optimal (Capon, or MVDR) weights for stacking sensors are the same formula, with C the covariance of the noise:

    w = C⁻¹ 1 / (1ᵀ C⁻¹ 1)

    The main results:

    • Tested against known answers. 59 tests. The rank correlations match SciPy's spearmanr and kendalltau, Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage matches scikit-learn's to within 10⁻⁶, and every cleaner gives the same answer on a covariance as on the matching correlation.
    • Removing the market reveals the sectors. On the 39 stocks, regressing out the market (the equal-weighted average return) raises the effective number of independent bets from about 7 to 15, and ordered by clustering the sectors appear as blocks down the diagonal. The top eigenvector, found without being told what the market is, correlates with the market return at 0.88.
    • Cleaning helps the matrix more than the portfolio. Built from one year of data and held for the next, a minimum-variance portfolio's realised risk was 1.48 times its prediction with the raw matrix and 1.41 to 1.44 times with the three cleaners.

    The other results:

    • Tail dependence. On their worst 5% of days, two banks (JPM and BAC) share about 70% of bad days, a bank and a utility (JPM and SO) about 20%, against 5% for independent assets.
    • The Epps effect. On simulated ticks with a true correlation of 0.70, sampling every tick measures 0.31. The Hayashi-Yoshida estimator, which uses only overlapping trade intervals, recovers 0.50 on irregular ticks.
    • Stacking. On a simulated array of 12 sensors with shared noise, the optimal weights gave about 5 dB of noise reduction, half the 11 dB that independent noise would allow.
    • Bugs caught by tests. My first nonlinear shrinkage was worse than no cleaning near q = 1, and an early clipping result that looked perfect came from applying a unit-variance noise band to a covariance matrix.

    We could extend the tests to the data the other projects use, such as solar sites and seismometer arrays, where the noise is not the independent Gaussian noise the Marchenko-Pastur band assumes.

    1. 01Correlation measures

      Correlation compresses a whole relationship into one number, and very different data can give similar numbers. The library has one interface for each measure:

      • Pearson. Uses the values, so a few extreme days can move it a long way.
      • Spearman and Kendall. Use only the ordering of the values.
      • Gaussian-rank. Maps each variable's ranks to a normal distribution, then takes Pearson.
      • Partial. The correlation left between two variables once all the others are held fixed.
      • Distance correlation. Zero only when two variables are independent, so it picks up relationships that are not straight lines.
      • Tail dependence and lead-lag. Whether two variables have their extremes together, and at what delay they move together.

      The first figure is four simulated pairs. The linear cloud has a Pearson correlation of 0.64. The second has no relationship at all except twelve extreme days, which give it 0.33. The two groups give 0.85 with neither group correlated inside itself, and the widening spread gives 0.54.     2. 02Estimators for messy data

      Real data rarely arrive on a shared clock or as independent samples, and the library has estimators for both problems.

      • Irregular trading times. Two assets do not trade at the same instant, so sampling very fast catches one just after it moved and the other just before. The measured correlation falls towards zero, which is called the Epps effect. The Hayashi-Yoshida estimator works from the raw timestamps, multiplying two returns into the covariance only when their time intervals overlap.
      • Samples that are not independent. Hourly solar output or daily returns carry over from one observation to the next, so there are fewer independent samples than data points. The effective sample size corrects for this from the autocorrelations, and the block bootstrap resamples whole blocks so the uncertainty keeps that memory.

      The figure is simulated ticks with a known true correlation of 0.70 (red dashed line), with a small error on every printed price. Sampling every tick measures 0.31, and the measured value rises with the sampling interval, reaching 0.71 at every 251 ticks. With each asset trading at its own random third of the ticks, Hayashi-Yoshida recovers 0.50 where plain sampling gives 0.31. The rest of the gap is information lost by the irregular sampling.

    3. 03Random-matrix cleaning

      With 39 stocks there are 741 correlations to estimate. Ten years of data (q = 0.016) is plenty, but one year (q = 0.16) is not, and much of what comes out is noise. Random-matrix theory gives the eigenvalue spectrum of pure noise, the Marchenko-Pastur law, so anything inside its band can be treated as noise. The library has four treatments:

      • Raw. The sample matrix, the baseline.
      • Clipping. Every eigenvalue inside the noise band replaced by one flat value.
      • Ledoit-Wolf. Every eigenvalue pulled the same fraction towards the average, with the fraction estimated from the data.
      • Nonlinear shrinkage. Each eigenvalue pulled by its own amount, using Ledoit & Wolf's 2020 analytical estimator.

      The first figure is the full-sample spectrum. The grey histogram is the measured eigenvalues, the red curve the pure-noise prediction with its band edge at 0.50 (dashed), and each blue line an eigenvalue beyond the band. The one at about 16 is the market, moving everything together. The second figure is the same for 2024 alone. The band now reaches 0.98, more of the spectrum lies inside it, and the effective rank of the raw matrix is 11.2 (11.8 clipped, 13.2 Ledoit-Wolf, 12.4 nonlinear shrinkage).

      The third figure is the usual test of whether cleaning helps a decision. A minimum-variance portfolio is built from one year of data and held for the next, over nine windows. Blue is the risk the portfolio predicted and orange the risk it delivered:

      • Raw. Realised risk 1.48 times the prediction.
      • Clipped, Ledoit-Wolf and nonlinear shrinkage. 1.44, 1.41 and 1.43 times.

      Every cleaner closes part of the gap and none closes most of it. This agrees with Bongiorno & Challet (2023), who find that the best estimator of the matrix is not the best for a minimum-variance portfolio, as the optimiser relies most on the small eigenvalues that are hardest to estimate. My first nonlinear shrinkage was worse than raw near q = 1, and an early clipping result that looked perfect came from applying a unit-variance noise band to a covariance of daily returns. The tests now check both.

    4. 04Removing the market

      Most of a stock correlation matrix is the market, since on a bad day almost everything falls together, and that one shared movement hides the structure underneath. There are two ways to take it out:

      • Regress out a known factor. Here the market's daily return, the equal-weighted average of the 39 stocks. This uses up one observation.
      • Remove the top mode. The strongest pattern of joint movement, the top eigenvector, without naming it. This uses up one variable.

      The Correlator tracks both and shifts the noise band to match. In the first figure, the three heatmaps share one clustered ordering:

      • Nothing removed. Everything is positively correlated, with blocks on top of a shared offset, and an effective rank of 6.9.
      • Market regressed out. The sectors stand out as blocks, utilities, energy, banks, tech, and the effective rank is 15.2.
      • Top mode removed. Much the same picture, effective rank 14.2. The removed mode correlates with the market return at 0.88, and the leftover correlations average −0.026, which is −1/(N − 1), the value removing a shared mode forces.

      The second figure is the Correlator's four standard panels for the market regressed out: the measured matrix, the final matrix in the same order, the spectrum after removal against the noise band (13 eigenvalues beyond it), and the cluster tree, where the branches below about 1.0 are the sectors. With ten years of data cleaning barely changes the matrix, and the measured bulk is wider than pure noise predicts, as fat tails and changing volatility do to real returns.

    5. 05Array stacking

      If many sensors record the same signal with their own noise, averaging them reduces the noise by √N, but only if the noise is independent.     An Asian option pays on the average price over a period rather than the price on one day. The contract priced here is a call on the average of ICE coffee, cocoa and sugar futures over the last three months of a six-month life (written 1 April 2025, averaging every business day from 1 July to 30 September), at the money, the way a commodity buyer who buys steadily through a season would hedge. Prices and volatility come from 2005-2025 daily settlements of the front-month futures.

    Futures have no drift under the pricing measure, so each simulated step is a volatility shock with a convexity correction, and the volatility σ(t) changes through the year:

    F(t + Δt) = F(t) exp(−½ σ(t)² Δt + σ(t) √Δt Z), σ(t) = level × seasonal(month of t)

    The geometric average of lognormal prices is itself lognormal, so the geometric Asian has an exact price. Monte Carlo prices the arithmetic Asian A and corrects it by the known error in the geometric one G, with β the regression slope between the two payoffs:

    Â = (1/n) Σ [ A_i − β (G_i − G_exact) ]

    Večeř (2001) turns the Asian into a PDE in one variable z, whatever the number of fixings, where q(t) is the share of fixings still to come:

    ∂u/∂t + ½ σ(t)² (q(t) − z)² ∂²u/∂z² = 0, u(T, z) = max(z, 0)

    Every price was built at least two independent ways and checked against the others. The main results:

    • Three methods agree. The simulated geometric Asian lands within 0.3 standard errors of its exact price, moment matching is within 0.05% of the control-variate Monte Carlo for coffee, and Večeř's PDE agrees with Monte Carlo to within one standard error (about 0.0003 on a price near 8) for six test options with flat and rising volatility.
    • The control variate does most of the work. It cuts the Monte Carlo variance by about 1,100 times for cocoa, 3,600 for coffee and 7,200 for sugar, so the standard error falls 33 to 85 times for the same paths.
    • The finite-difference solver is second order. On European options its error falls as grid size to the power −2.09.

    The other results, which are well-known results:

    • Averaging makes the option cheaper. The coffee Asian costs 32.0 US cents/lb against 39.6 for a European with the same strike, 0.81 of the price, and about the same ratio for cocoa and sugar.
    • The Asian's hedging risk comes early. Its delta fades as each fixing locks in, but rebalancing weekly costs the most in the month before averaging starts and the first month of it, while the European's risk is in its final month.

    • Softs volatility is only modestly seasonal. About ±10% around an average month, with coffee highest from July to November.

    Undergraduate numerical simulation work in C++. Laplace's equation is solved on the unit square, with fixed values on the boundary and fixed-potential objects inside the domain, first with Dirichlet and then with Neumann boundary conditions. Successive over-relaxation (SOR) is an iterative method: each grid point is repeatedly replaced by the average of its four neighbours, as in Gauss-Seidel, but each update is pushed beyond that average by a relaxation factor between 1 and 2, which cuts the number of sweeps needed to converge many times over.

    Undergraduate numerical simulation work in C++. A fourth-order Runge-Kutta integrator, written by hand and derived from the Taylor expansion, solves dx/dt = (t - 2)²(x + 1) and is checked against the exact solution. A fourth-order equation is then solved by rewriting it as four coupled first-order ones.

    The error falls as the fourth power of the step size (a fitted slope of 3.95, against 4 in theory). A boundary-value problem, where the solution is fixed at both ends, is solved by the shooting method: guess the starting slope, integrate across, and bisect on how far the end misses its target, which takes 25 iterations to reach x(10) = -1.
