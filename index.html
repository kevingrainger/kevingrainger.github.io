  • My undergraduate capstone at Trinity College Dublin, in Prof. Cormac McGuinness's Surface, Interface Physics & X-ray Spectroscopy Group. The aim was to find the band gap of metal-porphyrin chains and where their HOMO and LUMO states are located, using density functional theory in FHI-aims.

    The project found that the carbon bridge between unit cells in the 1D chain can change the HOMO-LUMO gap, where the frontier states are located and whether the chain is magnetic. In zinc chains a fully fused bridge carries the HOMO along the whole chain, while a weak bridge leaves it on each unit. With iron and vanadium centres the metal's d orbitals move into the gap and shorten it, so the frontier states are on the metal. Weakly bonded chains carry unpaired electrons on their carbon backbone, while more strongly fused chains tend to pair them up. The simulations point to the weak bridge keeping each unit's orbitals separate, so each porphyrin keeps the unpaired electrons it has as a single molecule, while fusing joins them into bands along the chain.

    Furthermore, it was found that an oxygen bound to the central metal can override the effect of the carbon bridge. The backbone spin disappears, leaving a single local spin on the metal-oxygen unit (S = 1 for iron, S = ½ for vanadium), and the HOMO-LUMO gap widens, from 0.34 to 0.64 eV in the fully fused iron chain.

    This points towards potential for chemical sensors and molecular electronics, both of great interest with porphyrins (Paolesse et al., 2017), given the graphene-like conduction that fused porphyrin chains can display (Gao et al., 2023).

    Porphyrins are flat rings of four pyrroles that hold a metal atom at the centre. They are the core of haemoglobin (iron) and chlorophyll (magnesium), and their spin and charge can be tuned through the central metal and whatever binds to it, which makes them candidates for molecular electronics, catalysis and chemical sensing. This project came about because on-surface synthesis can now make more complex porphyrin chains and ribbons, and it carries on from earlier studies of these larger porphyrin systems.

    The calculations ran in FHI-aims, the Fritz Haber Institute's density functional theory code, which expands the electron orbitals in numeric atom-centred functions. DFT replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density n(r) and solves the Kohn-Sham equations self-consistently, once for each spin channel:

    [ −½∇² + v_ext(r) + v_H[n](r) + v_xc[n](r) ] ψᵢ(r) = εᵢ ψᵢ(r), n(r) = Σᵢ |ψᵢ(r)|²

    Three design choices were varied: the carbon bridge between units (weak, strong or fully fused), the fused phenyl arms (none, four or two) and the central metal (zinc, iron, vanadium, cobalt and manganese, spanning the d shell from three electrons to ten). The main results:

    • The carbon bridge changes the gap and where the frontier states are located. In zinc chains the weak bridge leaves the HOMO on each unit and the fully fused bridge carries it along the whole chain. The gap is 0.31 eV weak, 0.05 eV strong and 0.78 eV fully fused, so more fusion narrows it only up to a point.
    • The metal's d orbitals influence the HOMO and LUMO. Zinc's full d shell leaves the carbon backbone on its own. In iron and vanadium chains the d levels lie among the carbon levels and shorten the gap, so the frontier states are on the metal. Manganese gives single-molecule gaps close to iron's (0.24 to 0.30 eV), while the cobalt centre had little effect on the density of states. A metal state at the edge of the gap, one per unit, points to electrons hopping from metal to metal rather than moving along a band, which would change how the chain conducts.
    • Oxidising the central metal fixes the spin. With one oxygen bound to the metal (Fe=O or V=O), the spin no longer changes with the bridge. Every iron chain has two unpaired electrons (total spin S = 1), shared between the iron and the oxygen, and every vanadium chain has one (S = ½), on the vanadium. The backbone spin, meaning unpaired spin on the chain's carbon, disappears. The gap widens and runs from about 0.15 eV (strong bridge) to 0.64 eV (fully fused).
    • The bridge structure also affects the magnetism of bare chains, meaning chains with no oxygen on the metal. With iron or vanadium centres, the weak bridge leaves about 2 μB of unpaired spin on the carbon backbone, and with the strong bridge the backbone carries almost none.
    • Cobalt and manganese chains did not converge. 6 of 45 cobalt runs finished and none of the seven manganese chains, so the chain results are for zinc, iron and vanadium. The likely reason is that both metals have several spin states close in energy, so the self-consistent loop kept switching between them instead of settling. Cobalt is also a hard case in experiment, where Sun et al. found its spin in PorA₂ screened by the gold surface (a Kondo effect).

    The gap and the frontier states are set by the bridge and the metal, and the spin by the metal-oxygen unit, so each could be tuned separately.

    1. 01Simulate a single molecule first

      We start with DFT simulations of a single porphyrin molecule, the unit the chains are built from. We run the plain ring (ZnP), then the ring with four fused phenyl arms (ZnTPP) and with two (ZnDPP). Zinc comes first because its full d shell has no unpaired electron, so the orbitals we see belong to the carbon framework alone.

      The plain ring is the form found in haem and chlorophyll. The phenyl arms are what on-surface synthesis fuses onto the ring when the molecules are heated on gold, and these are the molecules Sun et al. (2020) made and measured, as PorA₄ and PorA₂. Fusing the arms joins their carbon π orbitals to the ring's, so the molecule becomes a small piece of graphene-like carbon. This extended π system is what can carry charge along a chain.

      The figure shows HOMO−2 and LUMO+2 of each molecule, red and blue for the two signs of the wavefunction. On the plain ring the orbitals are on the pyrroles, and with the arms fused they spread out over the arms.

      The gaps show the main result. ZnP has a 2.77 eV gap and ZnTPP 1.17 eV, while ZnDPP's collapses to 0.10 eV. In a calculation without spin, every orbital must hold two electrons, one spin up and one spin down. A molecule with two unpaired electrons cannot be described that way, so the calculation shares the two electrons across two orbitals of almost the same energy, each half full, and the gap between those orbitals comes out close to zero. A near-zero gap in a run without spin is therefore the sign of two unpaired electrons, the open-shell PorA₂ that Sun et al. measured.

      Settings. PBE exchange-correlation functional, collinear spin where the metal has unpaired electrons, FHI-aims' numeric atom-centred basis, and every run converged to 10⁻⁵ in the density and 10⁻⁵ eV in the total energy. The chains are one unit cell repeated along the chain axis, sampled with 30 k-points.

    2. 02Swap in magnetic transition metals

      Next the zinc is replaced by transition metals with unpaired d electrons: iron, manganese, cobalt and vanadium. Each molecule is run spin-polarised, with separate spin-up and spin-down densities.

      The spin moment measures the unpaired electrons, the part of the molecule that is magnetic. Splitting the simulated spin into the part on the metal and the part on the ring (Mulliken analysis) shows whether the magnetism stays on the metal or spreads into the carbon that would carry a current in a chain. The gap is the energy needed to lift an electron into the first empty state, so a smaller gap points to easier conduction.

      The top panel shows the HOMO-LUMO gap for every metal and arm structure, on a log scale because the gaps run from 0.04 to 3.3 eV. Apart from cobalt's plain ring, every magnetic metal brings the gap down to between 0.04 and 0.34 eV, against 1.17 to 2.77 eV for zinc's closed-shell molecules. The bottom panels show the spin on the metal (green) and on the ring (purple) for each arm structure, joined by a line. Metal by metal:

      • Iron. Gap 0.04 eV plain, 0.34 eV with two arms, 0.30 eV with four. With two arms the ring picks up 1.9 μB aligned with the metal's 2.1, 4 μB in total. With four arms the ring spin points against the metal and the total cancels to zero.
      • Manganese. The largest metal moment, about 3.5 μB. The ring carries 1.4 μB aligned with two arms and 2.3 μB against with four. Gaps 0.24 to 0.30 eV.
      • Cobalt. Gap 3.33 eV on the plain ring, the widest of the magnetic metals, and 0.25 eV with four arms. The two-arm run did not finish.
      • Vanadium. Gaps 0.19 to 0.24 eV, the narrowest. With two arms little spin reaches the ring (0.2 μB), and with four the ring carries 2 μB aligned with the metal.
      • Zinc (no spin, for reference). Gaps 2.77, 1.17 and 0.10 eV.

      Sun et al. (2022) measured iron in PorA₂ on gold at about 3.7 μB, with the ring and metal spins aligned, and this run gives 4 μB, aligned. The four-arm result has not been measured, and PBE is known to overstate this kind of spin polarisation, so it is the less certain of the two.

    3. 03Orbitals molecule by molecule

      Every combination of metal and arms was run, which took a lot of numerical, convergence and speed tuning. This figure shows the standouts, five molecules with different metals and arm structures, and it is where the metals for the chains were chosen.

      For each one we look at the four orbitals around the gap: HOMO−1, HOMO, LUMO and LUMO+1. The HOMO holds the highest-energy electron and the LUMO is the first empty state an added electron would go into, so charge enters and leaves the molecule through these orbitals, and a current would pass through them. The question for each orbital is whether it is located on the metal or on the carbon. When the HOMO and LUMO are both on the carbon, the carbon π system sets the gap and the metal is a bystander. When one of them is on the metal, the metal's d levels have pushed in among the carbon levels, so the metal sets the gap, and changing the metal or what binds to it would tune it.

      The renders are from the thesis, red and blue for the two signs of the wavefunction. Row by row:

      • Zn-TPP. All four orbitals are spread over the ring and the four arms, with almost nothing on the zinc. This is the carbon-only reference.
      • Fe-TPP. The HOMO is a single round lobe on the iron with nothing on the carbon around it. Side-on it is iron's dz² orbital standing out of the ring plane. HOMO−1, LUMO and LUMO+1 are on the carbon, so iron's filled d level lies inside the carbon gap.
      • Fe-DPP. The same iron HOMO, with small lobes on the four nitrogens. The carbon orbitals around it stretch along the molecule's long axis, the direction the two fused arms point.
      • V-TPP. All four orbitals are on the ring and arms, as with zinc, but the vanadium joins in, with a lobe at the centre in HOMO−1 and LUMO.
      • V-DPP. The orbitals are on the ring and extend to the vanadium through the nitrogens, again stretched along the long axis.

      Iron gives the clearest case of a metal level inside the carbon gap, the most direct handle for tuning. Vanadium mixes metal and carbon. A mixed state means the metal's d orbital and the carbon π orbitals have combined into one orbital, so an electron in it is shared between the metal and the ring. The metal is then coupled into the carbon network that carries charge, which points towards band-like conduction through the metal rather than hopping from one metal to the next, and the thesis found the vanadium chains promising for charge conduction for this reason. Zinc is the carbon-only baseline. These three are the metals followed into the chains below.

    4. 04Linear porphyrin chains

      The molecules are then joined into linear chains. Each chain is simulated as one unit cell repeated along the chain axis, sampled with 30 k-points, and run spin-polarised for iron and vanadium. Three bridges were used. A weak bridge is a single carbon-carbon bond between arms, a strong bridge is three bonds after adding two carbons, and in the fully fused (extra-strong) chain the arms merge, so the backbone becomes a narrow graphene-like ribbon. A fourth chain joins two-arm (DPP) units end to end. These were the bridges partly or fully made by on-surface synthesis at the time.

      The orbitals are read by where they are located along the chain. Electrons can move smoothly along orbitals that run unbroken along the chain, as in an energy band, so a HOMO or LUMO spread over the whole backbone points to a chain that can carry current. An orbital that stays on each unit, or on each metal, means an electron has to hop from unit to unit, and the chain conducts poorly through that state. The gap is the energy needed to get an electron into the empty band.

      The figure shows the zinc chains' HOMO and LUMO, zinc first so the backbone can be seen on its own:

      • Weak bridge, gap 0.31 eV. The HOMO is on each porphyrin separately and breaks at the bridge, while the LUMO crosses it. An added electron could move along the chain through the LUMO, but a hole in the HOMO could not.
      • Strong bridge, gap 0.05 eV. The smallest gap of any chain. The HOMO stays on the outer arms of each unit, and the LUMO runs continuously through the bridges, so this is the zinc chain closest to conducting.
      • Fully fused, gap 0.78 eV. The HOMO runs the whole length of the chain without a break, a band along the ribbon. The gap opens again, as narrow graphene ribbons are known to do, so more fusion gives a smaller gap only up to a point.

      Then the magnetic metals go into the same chains (bare chains, spin-polarised):

      • Iron. Gap 0.21 eV weak, 0.13 eV strong, 0.34 eV fully fused and 0.29 eV DPP. The HOMO is the iron's dz² orbital, one lobe per unit with nothing linking them, and the LUMO is on the carbon.
      • Vanadium. Gap 0.18 eV weak and 0.22 eV DPP. In the weak chain the LUMO is a d orbital on each vanadium and the LUMO+1 is on the carbon rings. In the strong chain the HOMO is on the vanadium, and in the DPP chain the HOMO and LUMO are both on the vanadium, with the backbone barely involved (second figure).

      The main finding of this step is that in both iron and vanadium chains the metal's d orbital is at the frontier, inside the carbon gap. The first states an electron would enter or leave are on the metal and separate on each unit, and the carbon states that could carry a current lie just beyond them. The metal therefore sets the gap and the spin at the frontier, and changing what is bound to the metal is the way to tune them, which the oxygen test below does.

      The bare iron chains are the least certain numbers here. Their spin settled differently from chain to chain, so the result may depend on where each calculation started, and PBE is known to be unreliable for spin states in iron porphyrins.

    5. 05Orbitals along the chains

      This figure puts the HOMO and LUMO of four chains side by side, five units of each, to compare where the frontier orbitals are located once metals are in the chain:

      • Zinc, strong bridge. The HOMO is on each unit's pyrroles and arms and breaks at every bridge. The LUMO runs continuously through the bridges, a band along the whole chain, and this is the conducting state.
      • Iron, DPP chain. The HOMO is a row of iron dz² lobes, one per unit, with nothing in between, so a hole would stay on one iron. The LUMO is spread over the carbon of every unit and joins up between them.
      • Vanadium, weak bridge. The HOMO is a d orbital on each vanadium with some weight on the nitrogens around it. The LUMO is on the carbon rings of each unit, linked only through the single bond.
      • Vanadium, fully fused. The HOMO and LUMO are both on the vanadium, so the ribbon's carbon plays no part at the frontier.

      Zinc is the only chain with a continuous band at the frontier. Iron and vanadium put localised metal states at the edge of the gap, one per unit, with the carbon conduction states one step away. The metal states can be moved by binding something to the metal without rebuilding the backbone, and the next step does this with a single oxygen.

    6. 06Oxidising the central metal

      The last test came from the thesis conclusion, that these chains could be used for chemical sensing if a spin could be imposed through a ligand on the metal. Every iron and vanadium chain was run again with a single oxygen bound to the metal, 1.6 Å below the ring, which oxidises it to iron-oxo (Fe=O) or vanadyl (V=O). The question is what the oxygen does to the spin and to the gap.

      The figure shows the spin moment per unit cell of every chain, split into the part on the metal (green), the oxygen (grey) and the carbon backbone (purple). Faded bars are the bare chains from the previous steps and solid bars are the same chains with the oxygen bound.

      Without oxygen the spin is different in every chain. The weak iron chain has 1.8 μB on its carbon backbone, opposite to the metal, the weak vanadium chain has 2 μB on its backbone, aligned with the metal, and the chains with other bridges have almost none. The magnetism of a bare chain depends on the bridge, and for iron possibly on how the calculation was started. Oxidising the metal removes those differences:

      • Iron (Fe=O). 2 μB in every chain, 1.2 on the iron and 0.8 on the oxygen, the S = 1 known for iron(IV)-oxo porphyrins. Iron shares its spin with the oxygen.
      • Vanadium (V=O). 1 μB in every chain, almost all of it on the vanadium, the S = ½ of a vanadyl porphyrin. The oxygen takes almost no spin (−0.13 μB).
      • Backbone. The spin on the carbon backbone falls below 0.08 μB in every chain.
      • Gap. The gap does not collapse. It widens in every chain (second figure), for iron from 0.21 to 0.25 eV (weak), 0.13 to 0.15 eV (strong), 0.34 to 0.64 eV (fully fused) and 0.29 to 0.34 eV (DPP), and for vanadium from 0.18 to 0.27 eV (weak) and 0.22 to 0.48 eV (DPP).

      This gives one predictable local spin per unit, whatever the bridge, on a carbon backbone that is no longer magnetic. The magnetic behaviour is fixed by the metal-oxygen unit, and the charge-carrying behaviour is left to the backbone and the bridge. This is the arrangement the thesis conclusion asked for in a chemical sensor.

    7. 07Density of states with and without oxygen

      The density of states counts how many electron states a chain has at each energy. Here it is plotted against energy relative to the chemical potential (the dotted line at 0), the energy that separates the filled states below from the empty states above. The chemical potential is not the gap itself. It is a single energy, and in these chains it lies inside the gap, between the HOMO below it and the LUMO above it, so the states closest to 0 on either side are the ones that take part in conduction and in any change of spin. Spin-up states are drawn above the axis and spin-down below, with the metal in green, oxygen in black and carbon shaded.

      • Vanadium, weak bridge, bare (top left). Sharp vanadium peaks just below 0, mixed in with carbon states. The metal's d electrons are at the frontier.
      • Vanadium, weak bridge, with oxygen (bottom left). The vanadium peaks move away from 0. One sharp spin-up peak is left 0.75 eV below, with no spin-down partner, which is the single unpaired electron of S = ½. Near 0 there are only carbon states.
      • Iron, fully fused, bare (top right). Iron peaks in both spin channels within about half an electronvolt of 0, mixed in with the carbon.
      • Iron, fully fused, with oxygen (bottom right). Iron and oxygen peaks appear together, 0.5 to 0.75 eV below 0 and about 1.2 eV above, and the carbon states around 0 open into a wider gap (0.34 to 0.64 eV).

      Binding the oxygen pulls the metal's states away from the chemical potential. The spin is then held in the metal-oxygen unit, below the frontier, and the states at the frontier, the ones that would carry a current, are the carbon bands. This means a current along the chain would pass through the carbon backbone while the spin stays on the metal-oxygen unit, so changing what is bound to the metal changes the spin without cutting off the path the current takes.

    8. 08All the design variables together

      The project ran many combinations: five metals, three arm structures, three bridges plus the DPP chain, with and without spin, and with and without oxygen on the metal. This figure gathers the runs that finished.

      The top left panel is the HOMO-LUMO gap of each molecule, by metal and arms. The top right panel is the gap of every chain by bridge, for zinc and for iron and vanadium, bare and with oxygen, with the backbone spin written under each gap. Both use the same log colour scale, dark for small gaps. The bottom left panel is the spin on the ring of each molecule, red aligned with the metal and blue opposite, with the total spin in each cell.       The gaps carry the main result. In single molecules every magnetic metal narrows the gap compared with zinc, to between 0.04 and 0.34 eV, apart from cobalt's plain ring. In chains the bridge sets the gap. The strong bridge gives the smallest gaps (0.05 eV for zinc, 0.13 to 0.21 eV with a metal) and the fully fused bridge the largest (0.34 to 0.78 eV), and oxidising the metal widens the gap in every chain. For conduction alone, the strongly bridged zinc chain is the best candidate, with the smallest gap and a LUMO that runs continuously along the chain.

      In the spin panels, the arms affect the ring's spin in single molecules. Two arms align it with the metal, and four mostly turn it against it. In bare chains (no oxygen on the metal) the bridge affects whether the backbone carries a spin, and only the weak bridge leaves a large one. With oxygen bound the backbone spin is zero in every chain, and the filled points on the right all lie on the zero line. The gap is set by the bridge and the spin by the metal-oxygen unit.

      The most promising combination for further work is the vanadyl (V=O) chain. It has one S = ½ spin per unit on the vanadium, almost nothing on the oxygen or the backbone, and a gap that the bridge moves from 0.21 to 0.60 eV. Of the two oxidised chains it is also the more certain result, since PBE is unreliable for iron spin states. The next calculation would double the unit cell so that neighbouring spins can point opposite ways, and measure the coupling between them through each bridge.

      Much of this project is chemical combinatorics, with metal, arms, bridge and the ligand on the metal each multiplying the number of runs, and each run needing its own convergence tuning. With AI now being applied to DFT, I'd like to take this further with a lightweight, physics-informed model that screens the combinations and sends only the most promising to full DFT. The pieces exist. Neural networks already predict DFT spin-state splittings of transition-metal complexes (Janet & Kulik, 2017) and the gaps of over 12,000 porphyrins (Li et al., 2018), and DeepH learns the DFT Hamiltonian itself, so the orbitals of large structures can be predicted without the self-consistent loop (Li et al., 2022). The catch, which Kulik's group also found, is that the ranking depends on the functional the training data came from, so PBE's weakness for iron spin states would carry over into the model.

    9. 09Work since the capstone

      The capstone was finished in June 2025. A few studies since then are close to it. They are measurements and calculations on real surfaces, where the capstone was a set of PBE calculations on chains in vacuum, without the gold surface, so where they overlap it is in outline only.

      • Vanadyl porphyrins as qubits. Vanadyl (V=O) porphyrins are studied as molecular spin qubits. Privitera et al. (2026) joined two vanadyl porphyrins through a porphyrin bridge, and their spins were independent until light excited the bridge, which coupled them. Vanadyl porphyrins held in a zirconium framework kept their spin coherence (about 1 μs) up to 180 K (Inorg. Chem., 2026). This fits the result here that the V=O spin stays on the vanadium whatever the bridge, though none of this work uses fused chains.
      • Porphyrins on graphene nanoribbons. Xiang et al. (2025) attached metal porphyrins along the edge of a zigzag graphene nanoribbon on gold and measured exchange coupling between distant metal centres, carried by the ribbon's π electrons. In their ribbons the metal's spin stays on the metal. My one Fe=O porphyrin set into a ribbon (the figure) put the spin on the ribbon's carbon instead. It is a different structure and a single PBE run, so I would not read much into the difference.
      • Fused porphyrin chains as spin chains. On gold, fused porphyrin chains of one to seven units each carry an unpaired π electron, and the way the units are fused changes the coupling between neighbouring spins (JACS, 2025). This is close to the bare-chain result here, where the weak bridge leaves unpaired spin on the backbone and the fused bridges mostly do not.

  • Xylella fastidiosa is a bacterium that blocks a tree's water vessels. Since it was found near Gallipoli in 2013 it has killed millions of olive trees in southern Italy, and Schneider et al. (2020) estimate it could cost Italy €1.9 to 5.2 billion over 50 years if the dead groves are not replanted. This is what got me interested in modelling it. This project is a recreation of the agent-based model of Fierro, Liccardo & Porcelli (2019), in which spittlebugs carry the bacterium from tree to tree, rebuilt light enough to be run and tuned many more times. The aim was to bring in percolation theory and look for planting spacings and felling patterns that break up the spread.

    Via stochastic simulation, it was found that outbreaks are all or nothing. 39 of 40 simulated outbreaks infected the whole grove, and below roughly 16% chance of infection per spittlebug visit, outbreaks die out. With trees spaced 6 to 8 m apart, and spittlebug numbers per tree kept down by managing the ground cover, the infection no longer spreads through the whole grove. None of the felling patterns tried slowed the spread.

    Xylella is spread by meadow spittlebugs, which feed on xylem sap, the water moving up a plant's stem. In the model the season sets where they are. When the olive trees put out tender new sprouts in spring (days 90 to 185 of the year), spittlebugs move up from the grass and shrubs into the trees. Once the sprouts harden they move back down to the ground cover, and each spring a new generation emerges uninfected. On a tree an infected spittlebug can pass on the bacterium, and an infected tree shows symptoms about two years later, after which spittlebugs avoid it. Current control in Apulia removes infected trees and the host plants around them, and tills the ground cover in spring to kill spittlebug nymphs, which cut their numbers by about 60% in a field trial (Sanna et al., 2021).

    The paper's grove has about a million spittlebugs per hectare, 810,000 on a 90 × 90 m grove, far too many to run many times. I implemented a lighter version of the model of Fierro, Liccardo & Porcelli (2019) that moves every spittlebug at once instead of one at a time (about 95 times faster) and simulates 810 of them, 0.1% of the paper's number. I checked my results against a direct implementation of the paper's rules over 12 + 200 runs. Later I also tested letting each simulated spittlebug stand for a group, so that a small swarm could behave like a denser one.

    An infected spittlebug on a healthy tree infects it with probability p per visit, and the tree shows symptoms after a delay τ drawn from a normal distribution:

    P(infection on a visit) = p, τ ~ N(730, 100²) days

    Runs at different numbers of spittlebugs are combined with multilevel Monte Carlo (Giles, 2008), where Q is the share of trees infected after a year and level l simulates 405 × 2ˡ spittlebugs:

    E[Q_L] = E[Q_0] + Σₗ E[Q_l − Q_{l−1}]

    The results were mixed, but there were two thresholds of note:

    • Infection per visit. A spittlebug stays on a tree for about 18 days at a time in the paper's model. Via stochastic simulation, it was found that if its chance of passing on the infection during that stay is below roughly 16%, outbreaks die out, and above about 29% they kill the whole grove.
    • Spacing. The spacing of the olive trees can act as a percolation threshold. With the number of spittlebugs per tree held fixed, the infection stops spreading through the whole grove once trees are 6 to 8 m apart. Real groves are often planted that far apart, so the spacing is realistic and the result fairly obvious, but it only works if spittlebug numbers per tree stay down, which means managing the ground cover. With spittlebugs per hectare held fixed instead, wider spacing adds as many spittlebugs as the distance removes, and 69-85% of trees are infected at every spacing.

    The other results:

    • Outbreaks are all or nothing. 39 of the 40 runs killed the whole grove, half of it within about 2.3 years, and one never started. I expected dying trees to slow the spread into some kind of equilibrium, or culling and certain felling patterns to stop it, but the simulations found none of these.
    • Felling. Many felling patterns were tried, varying the radius and how quickly infected trees are found. None of this slowed the disease spread.
    • The two-year delay makes intervening difficult. A tree takes about two years to show symptoms, which leaves plenty of time for the bacterium to reach the trees around it before anyone can see it. To stop an outbreak the chance of infection per visit itself has to be low.
    • Multilevel Monte Carlo gave some slightly unexpected results. Multilevel Monte Carlo estimates the answer for many spittlebugs as the answer for few, plus a set of corrections, each the difference between a run with twice as many spittlebugs and a run with half. A correction is only cheap to pin down if the two runs in a pair follow nearly the same outbreak, so each pair is built from shared random numbers. The fine run takes the coarse run's spittlebugs, with the same starting positions and the same random choice at every step, and adds the same number again with new random numbers. Both runs also share the first infected spittlebug and each tree's symptom delay. This coupling is how standard multilevel Monte Carlo is meant to work, and it held here, with paired runs correlating at 0.62-0.78 against about zero for pairs that only share a starting seed. It still saved no time, needing about 2.2 hours of computer time against 1.7 for plain Monte Carlo, because the corrections did not shrink at finer levels and a finer run cost only about 1.6 times more.
    • The number of spittlebugs simulated sets the speed of the outbreak. The share of trees infected after a year rises from 1% with 405 spittlebugs to 49% with 12,960. The issue comes from approximating the paper's 810,000 spittlebugs with 810, which makes the outbreak slower than the paper's density would. Letting each simulated spittlebug stand for a group, p → 1 − (1 − p)ᵏ, did not fix this, because the bottleneck is how many trees a swarm reaches, not the chance of infection per visit. The main fix is to set the spittlebug density correctly, so the simulation represents the much larger real swarm, and to fit it to real groves alongside the infection rate.
    • Dead-tree counts alone can't identify the infection rate. To test whether the rate could be learned from images, we ran a twin experiment, where a simulated grove with a known rate (0.5) stands in for real data. Every 120 days its symptomatic trees were observed, each seen only 80% of the time, as in aerial images. A particle filter, which runs many copies of the model (here 32 groves, each with its own guess of the rate) and keeps the copies that best match each new observation, could not recover the rate. After six years its 90% range was still about 0.1 to 0.9. A direct check without the filter gave the same answer. Groves run at every rate from 0.1 to 1.0 matched the observed counts about equally well, because above the threshold every outbreak kills almost the whole grove (253 to 255 of 256 trees at every rate).

    We could fit the model to real image sequences instead, such as the Gallipoli grove that went from full canopies in 2014 to dead trees by 2026. Comparing which trees die and in what order, with a Wasserstein distance (how far one pattern of dead trees has to move to match another), would carry the information the counts lack, and multilevel Monte Carlo would keep the many runs affordable. With the rates fitted, we could then design felling and ground-cover patterns.

    2. 02Season encoding

      Meadow spittlebugs spend most of the year in the grass and shrubs and move onto the olive trees in spring, when the trees put out tender new sprouts. The model encodes this with two seasons, using the paper's movement rules:

      • Tender sprouts (days 90 to 185). A spittlebug next to a tree always climbs on (probability 1.0) and rarely leaves (0.005 per attempted step).
      • Hard sprouts (the rest of the year). Spittlebugs are pushed off the trees (1.0) and cannot climb back on (0).
      • Every step. A spittlebug tries to move half the time, 0.5 between grass cells and 0.3 within a tree, with 30 steps a day (the paper uses 48).
      • Each spring (day 120). A new generation of uninfected adults replaces the old one, since nymphs do not carry the bacterium through moulting.
      • Picking up the bacterium. A spittlebug's chance of being infected by a tree rises with how long the tree has been colonised, reaching 1 after about 90 days.

      My first implementation started the grove in the tender season on day 0, which should be the hard season. On day 0 the infected spittlebug could climb a tree and infect it a full season early, which raised the share infected at day 600 from 20% to 27%, 38% more (p < 0.001). Every result from here uses the corrected start.

    6. 06Multilevel Monte Carlo

      Simulating more spittlebugs is more realistic but slower. Multilevel Monte Carlo (Giles, 2008) estimates the answer at the most detailed level as a cheap answer at a coarse level plus a set of corrections. Here level 0 simulates 405 spittlebugs and each level doubles it, up to 12,960 at level 5, and each correction is the difference between the share infected at one level and the level below.

      Each correction comes from a pair of runs, one fine (2N spittlebugs) and one coarse (N). If the two runs were independent, their difference would be as noisy as the runs themselves and nothing would be saved. So the fine run is built from the coarse one. Its first N spittlebugs are the coarse run's spittlebugs, with the same starting positions and the same random choice at every step, and the other N have their own random numbers. Both runs also share the first infected spittlebug and each tree's symptom delay, so they follow nearly the same outbreak and their difference is small. This coupling is the standard way multilevel Monte Carlo is used, and the blue line in the second figure is the uncoupled alternative, where the pair only shares a starting seed. 30 pairs were run at each level.

      • The coupling held. Paired runs correlate at 0.62-0.78, against about zero when they only share a seed.
      • No saving. Pinning the share infected to within one point needed about 2.2 hours against 1.7 for plain Monte Carlo at the finest level. Multilevel Monte Carlo pays when the corrections shrink at finer levels and fine runs are much dearer than coarse ones, and neither held here.
      • The answer depends on the number of spittlebugs. The share infected after a year rises from 1% with 405 to 49% with 12,960, so the simulation has not settled at any of these numbers.

      The first figure shows one pair. The coarse run's 3,240 spittlebugs (navy) appear unchanged in the fine run, which adds 3,240 more (orange). The two runs share their first infections, but the fine run's extra spittlebugs then reach many more trees (25% against 1% after a year), so the difference between them stays large, which is why the method saved no time. The grey line is a fine run that only shares the seed.

The method could pay once the spittlebug density is set to a realistic value where the answer settles, but density is still the main issue.

    How the data flows
    1. Grove. A 90 × 90 NumPy array of cell types (shrub 0, tree 1, empty 2), with trees planted as 3 × 3 blocks every 5 cells. Each tree's health, infection day and symptom day are held in dicts keyed by tree position.
    2. Vectors. The spittlebugs are stored as parallel arrays with one entry per insect: x and y positions (int16), and infected and alive flags (bool). The paper's density is sampled down for speed and transmission scaled up to compensate.
    3. Time step. 48 steps per day. Each step, every insect moves to a neighbouring cell with probabilities set by the cell type and the season (tender sprouts before day 185, hard after). An insect on an infected tree may pick up the bacterium; an infected insect on a healthy tree may pass it on.
    4. Record. Each day, the S, I and R tree counts and the infected-insect fraction are appended to history lists. The transmission log adds one row per infection: source tree, target tree, day.
    5. Theory. From the log, transmissions are binned by distance to give T(d), and the front position is fitted against time to give its speed. These feed the percolation calculation and the front-speed formula, which return predicted outbreak size and a predicted timeline.
    6. Output. A three-panel figure (grove map, SIR curves with seasonal phases, insect infection rate), with the theory curves overlaid on the simulated ones.

I have always been interested in how ecosystems collapse, and coral reefs are a clear case. In the global bleaching event of 2023-25, about 84% of the world's reef area had heat stress high enough to bleach coral (NOAA Coral Reef Watch, 2025).     Read the write-up and code

    The reef is a lattice of patches, each healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock or macroalgae (seaweed). Each day every patch may propose a change of state, and what it changes to depends on the water temperature and on what its neighbours are. Two pieces of biology make collapse possible. Coral under prolonged heat dies, and seaweed grows over the dead coral unless grazing fish clear it, with the same fish spread over more seaweed as coral dies. This is the feedback Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007) use to make real reefs bistable.

    A proposed change of state is accepted with the Metropolis rule, where ΔE is the change in the patch's energy and β sets how noisy the reef is:

    P(accept) = min(1, exp(−β ΔE))

    A lower energy is a more likely state. For a healthy patch, f_H(x) is the share of its neighbours that are healthy, J is the neighbour coupling (divided by the number of neighbours, so a longer range changes the reach of the interaction and not its total strength), ε_H is the baseline preference for healthy coral, s is the stress sensitivity and D₅₀(x) is the patch's own heat tolerance:

    E_H(x) = −J f_H(x) − ε_H + s · max(DHW(x) − D₅₀(x), 0)

    Heat stress is measured as NOAA Coral Reef Watch measures it (Skirving et al., 2020), in degree heating weeks (DHW). HS is the sea temperature above the warmest-month mean (MMM), and DHW adds it up over the last 12 weeks, counting only days at least 1 °C above:

    HS(t) = SST(t) − MMM, DHW(t) = (1/7) Σ_{last 84 days} HS, HS ≥ 1 °C

    The model was fitted to 65 AIMS site surveys of Scott Reef in April 2016 by simulation-based inference, and the fit was checked on 800 simulated reefs it had not seen. The main results:

    • Every interaction range has hysteresis. Warmed and then cooled back, the reef comes back along a lower path. Once heat kills enough coral, seaweed grows over it, and the coral only returns once the water is cooled well below where it was lost. The gap between the warming and cooling paths is about 60% wider when every patch feels the whole reef (mean field) than when it feels its four nearest neighbours, and only the mean-field reef stays degraded (0.49 cover against 0.9) when cooled back to the baseline. With short-range coupling, surviving patches reseed the patches next to them.
    • Two reefs fit the surveys. Via simulation-based inference, it was found that a sensitive reef with mean tolerance 4.5 DHW (37% of the posterior) and a tolerant reef at 12.7 DHW with a sharper response to stress (63%) reproduce the April surveys equally well. The survey was taken at 16.5 DHW, far beyond both, so it cannot separate how tolerant the corals are from how steeply they respond once past it.
    • The tipping point. Held a fixed amount above the warmest-month mean, the sensitive reef tips at +1.1 to 1.2 °C and the tolerant one at +1.4 to 1.7 °C. Neither recovers on cooling in any of the 40 runs.

    The other results:

    • Warming speed has little effect. In a grid of heatwaves, how hot the water gets changes the coral left afterwards, and how fast it warms changes it very little.
    • No early warning. Rising variance and autocorrelation (Scheffer et al., 2009) and spatial patchiness gave no warning, because under slow warming the coral cover falls gradually rather than suddenly.
    • The data. My first fit was to a bleaching curve that turned out to be drawn by hand, with temperatures that do not match Scott Reef (a correlation of 0.12 with NOAA's record). Those fits were removed and the model refitted to the AIMS surveys.
    • Dead coral by early April. The sensitive reef has lost about half its living coral by 5 April (16-66% across draws), and the tolerant one none. AIMS reported widespread mortality at Scott Reef on 5 April 2016, which agrees with the sensitive reef, though this field report was not used in the fit.

    We could tell the two reefs apart with a single survey from February, when the sensitive reef has 59% of its coral bleached and the tolerant one 26%. Repeated spatial surveys of a reef through a whole season would let a lattice model like this be fitted properly, and larvae arriving from other reefs would let a dead reef recover, which the model cannot yet do.

    1. 01Rebuilding the reef model

      We start from my undergraduate Potts model of a reef, rewritten in NumPy so that every patch updates at once, about 9 times faster than the original. The reef is a disc of rock on a 100 × 100 lattice surrounded by open water, with 75% of the rock starting as healthy coral. Each patch is healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock or macroalgae, and each day it may propose a change of state that is accepted with the Metropolis rule. Strictly this is a Potts-inspired stochastic cellular automaton rather than a Potts model in the equilibrium sense, since death and colonisation only go one way.

      In my original code coral could never die. Its death rule needed heat stress above a cap the stress could never reach, so bleached coral always recovered and the reef could never get stuck. Two rules were added, following Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007):

      • Mortality. Bleached coral dies at a daily rate (3%) that rises with how long the heat has lasted.
      • Seaweed and grazing. Macroalgae colonise bare rock, faster next to existing macroalgae, and grazing fish clear them. The same fish are spread over all the non-coral area, so grazing weakens as coral dies.

      The figure is one reef warmed slowly from +2.0 to +4.5 °C above the bleaching baseline, with healthy coral in orange, bleached coral in white, bare rock in grey and macroalgae in green. At +2.0 °C the reef is mostly healthy. By +3.3 and +3.8 °C much of it is bleached, with macroalgae appearing on the dead coral, and by +4.5 °C macroalgae cover most of the reef.     2. 02Interaction range and hysteresis

      The best-known reef models are mean-field, where every patch feels the average of the whole reef. From physics we know that mean-field models exaggerate how sudden and permanent a change of state is, because they leave out the local survivors that can reseed the patches around them. To test this, the reef was run with four interaction ranges, its 4 nearest neighbours, 8, 12, and every patch on the reef (mean field). The neighbour coupling is divided by the number of neighbours, so only the reach of the interaction changes and its total strength stays the same.

      To look for a tipping point we warm the water in steps of 0.25 °C from 0 to +4 °C, holding each step for 60 days, then cool it back down the same way, averaging three runs at each range. If the reef comes back along the same path there is no tipping point. If it comes back along a lower path, the reef has hysteresis, which means it got stuck in the degraded state.

      In the figure the solid lines are the healthy coral cover on warming and the dashed lines on cooling, one colour per interaction range, and the shaded area between them is the hysteresis loop.

      • Every range has hysteresis. At every range the cooling path is below the warming path from +4 °C down to about +0.75 °C. At +1 °C, for example, the reef has about 0.80 cover on warming and 0.52-0.63 on cooling.
      • Short ranges recover at the baseline. Cooled all the way back to 0 °C, the reefs with 4 to 12 neighbours return to 0.88-0.91 cover, close to where they started.
      • Mean field does not recover. The mean-field reef comes back to only 0.49 cover at 0 °C, and its loop is about 60% wider than the reef with 4 neighbours.

      This means the mean-field model exaggerates collapse, as the physics suggested. With short-range coupling, surviving patches reseed the cells next to them, which softens the collapse.

      The second figure gives the width of each loop, the area between the warming and cooling paths, with the change from the 4-neighbour reef in brackets. Widening the range from 4 to 12 neighbours adds 18%, and the jump to mean field adds 64%.

    3. 03Warming speed and early warnings

      Two further questions were asked of the same model. The first is whether a reef warmed quickly fares worse than one warmed slowly to the same peak. The reef was warmed to peaks from +3.5 to +5.0 °C over 5 to 300 days, held for 240 days and cooled for 250.

      In the first figure the colour is the healthy coral cover at the end, the x-axis the days taken to reach the peak and the y-axis the peak. The colour bands run flat across the map, so how hot the water gets changes the outcome and how fast it warms changes it very little. The black lines are the heat added up over the whole run (degree-weeks above 1 °C), and the bands cut across them, so the total heat does not set the outcome either. The temperature the reef is held at does.

      The second question is whether a collapse can be seen coming. Early-warning signals (Scheffer et al., 2009) are statistics that tend to rise before a sudden transition. Three were tracked, the variance and the lag-1 autocorrelation of the coral cover over time (how much one day's value carries into the next), and Moran's I, a measure of how patchy the reef is in space. The reef was warmed slowly from +2.0 to +4.5 °C over 900 days.

      In the second figure the top panel is the coral cover, and the three below are the variance, the autocorrelation and Moran's I, each over a rolling window. The dashed line marks where the cover falls to half its starting value. None of the three rises before it, and the variance falls as the cover falls. This is because under slow warming the cover slides down gradually, and these signals are built to detect an approaching jump. In this model the tipping shows up on the way back, in the hysteresis loops of the previous step.

    4. 04Real heat stress and corals that differ

    5. 05Simulation-based inference

      A lattice model has no likelihood to write down, as it can only be simulated. I first tried tuning it with Optuna, and it was hard to get anything that behaved like a real reef rather than collapsing completely. Instead I used simulation-based inference (Cranmer, Brehmer & Louppe, 2020), which learns the link between parameters and data from simulations:

      • Simulate. 8,000 reefs were run through the 2015-16 heat record, with four parameters drawn from wide ranges: mean tolerance μ_D (2-16 DHW), its spread σ_D (0.25-5 DHW), the stress sensitivity s (0.1-1.5) and the noise β (0.05-0.5).
      • Survey. Each simulated reef was surveyed as AIMS did, at 65 random sites on 16 April, binned into the same four categories.
      • Learn. A mixture density network (Bishop, 1994) was trained to map a survey to a probability distribution over the four parameters, as a sum of Gaussians. I wrote it in NumPy with the gradients derived by hand.

      Before using the real survey, the inference was checked on 800 simulated reefs it had not seen. For every parameter, the 90% interval contained the true value 89.5-90.4% of the time, so the uncertainty it reports can be trusted. The fitted model predicts on average 1.4, 6.5, 20 and 37 sites in the four categories, against 0, 6, 24 and 35 observed.

      In the first figure, the left panel is the posterior over mean tolerance and stress sensitivity, darker where more likely. It has two modes:

      • A sensitive reef. Mean tolerance about 4.5 DHW and a low stress sensitivity (37% of the posterior).
      • A tolerant reef. Mean tolerance about 12.7 DHW and a sharper response to stress (63%).

One snapshot taken that far past both thresholds cannot separate how tolerant the corals are from how steeply they respond once past it. In the second figure, the lower panel is the bleached share of living coral in each mode (median and 10-90% range) and the vertical lines the 65 surveys.       The third figure is the check on held-out reefs at every interval level, with the share of reefs whose interval holds the true value on the y-axis. All four parameters lie on the dashed diagonal, which is what a well-calibrated posterior gives.

    6. 06Scott Reef's tipping point

      With the fitted parameters the tipping question can be asked again, with error bars. For 40 parameter sets from the posterior, 20 from each mode, the water is held a fixed amount above the warmest-month mean (29.5 °C) for 120 days, stepped up 0.1 °C at a time, then stepped back down. The tipping point is where living coral first falls below half its starting value.

      In the figure each line is one parameter set, purple for the sensitive reef and green for the tolerant one. The solid lines are living coral on warming and the dashed lines on cooling.

      • Sensitive reef. Tips at +1.1 to +1.2 °C, about 30.6-30.7 °C.
      • Tolerant reef. Tips at +1.3 to +1.8 °C, most draws between +1.4 and +1.7 °C.
      • Cooling. No run recovers. Every dashed line stays near zero.

      Neither reef comes back because the model has no coral larvae arriving from outside the lattice, so a reef that has died has nothing to regrow from. Real reefs do recover slowly from larvae carried in from other reefs, so the hysteresis here is an upper bound. The model also gives a check against the field. By 5 April the sensitive reef has lost about half its living coral (16-66% across draws) and the tolerant one none, and AIMS reported widespread mortality at Scott Reef on 5 April 2016. That report was not used in the fit, but it agrees with the sensitive reef.

      The second figure plots each draw's tipping point against its mean tolerance. Within the sensitive reef the tipping point barely moves (+1.1 to +1.2 °C for tolerances of 3 to 8 DHW), while within the tolerant reef it rises from +1.3 °C at 8.7 DHW to +1.8 °C at 15.7 DHW.

    7. 07Next steps: more reef data

      The data were the hardest part of this project. Most of the time went on a reef that collapsed totally and would not behave like a real one, and on finding that the curve I had calibrated to was not data. The nearest-neighbour analogy is also only loosely true. Corals do help their neighbours (shade, shelter, larvae settling nearby), but the energies in the model are chosen rather than derived, so the hysteresis results should be read as qualitative.

      • An earlier survey. A single survey from February 2016 would tell the sensitive and tolerant reefs apart, but the database has none for Scott Reef that year. The figure shows each posterior draw's bleached share on 1 February (circles) and 16 April (squares). In April the two reefs overlap, at medians of 82% and 84%, while on 1 February they are at 59% and 26%.
      • Repeated spatial surveys. Surveys of the same sites through a whole season, with their positions on the reef, would let a lattice model be fitted with machine learning rather than tuned by hand.
      • Larval supply. Larvae arriving from neighbouring reefs are the main missing biology, and would let a dead reef recover.

      We could also look at the mathematics of these systems, when a lattice model has a sharp transition and what sets its order, alongside the applied side.

    This continues the theme of earth systems as lattices, and their links back to physics. Earth-observation data comes as rasters, plants interact with their neighbours, and soil health and soil moisture behave like underlying fields, so the parallels with lattice models in physics are worth exploring. Desertification is a pressing climate problem I have always been interested in, and Kéfi et al.'s 2007 Nature paper on vegetation patterns and imminent desertification inspired me to look at it this way. The model is their probabilistic cellular automaton: each cell is vegetated, empty but fertile, or degraded, and bare soil recovers faster next to vegetation (facilitation), the same neighbour coupling as the coral reef's Potts model. With strong facilitation the system shows hysteresis: recovering from desert takes much more rainfall than the collapse did. The mean-field theory, which ignores space, overstates how stuck it gets.

    The patch structure gives a much clearer early warning than total cover. As rainfall falls, the largest patch drops from 90% of all vegetation to 6% while cover is still near a half, well before the collapse, so tracking patch sizes in satellite imagery would warn of decline where average greenness would not. I drove the lattice with real rainfall at two Sahel sites: Gourma in Mali, which re-greened after the droughts, and Fakara in Niger, which did not. The rainfall data was a problem. Sahel rain falls in short, local storms, and averaging it over large grid boxes damps the signal: ERA5 came out about 35% too dry and agreed poorly with IMERG, so the runs use CHIRPS, which blends satellite estimates with rain gauges. As a stress test, the 1980s drought takes the lattice just below its tipping point, but too briefly to collapse it. Rainfall also predicted re-greening and decline less well than I expected: at Fakara greenness hardly follows the rain, and land use decides more of it.

    Played year by year as a strip of landscape beside each site's satellite record, the lattice loses about a quarter of its cover through the 1980s at both sites; MODIS greenness follows it loosely at Gourma and not at all at Fakara. Over 2000-2025 the lattice gets the direction of change right at Gourma but too weakly (+2.8% a decade against +9.3% in MODIS), and drifts down at Fakara where the satellites see none. To find what else matters, I took the question to 223 sites across the Sahel, removed the part of each site's greening that its rain explains (RESTREND), and related what was left to cropland, livestock, population and soil. 89% of the sites greened beyond their rain over 2001-2024, and the more used land greened less: one standard deviation more of a combined land-use index goes with about 1.1 points a decade less re-greening. Cropping, grazing and people rise together, so the data can't separate them, soil adds little, and most of the differences between sites remain unexplained. Following cropland year by year with MODIS didn't settle it: at 500 m it misses most Sahel fields. I then fitted the lattice to MODIS, with greenness as this year's grass plus the lattice's slow cover and a degradation rate that changes with land use after 1985. It still doesn't match well: at Gourma rain does almost all the work, and at Fakara nothing fits the unseen years. Fitting dry-season greenness (mostly shrubs and trees) across all 223 sites with one set of rates did better: the lattice ranks the sites' greenness and its trend about as well as smoothed rain does (0.64 and 0.49 against 0.67 and 0.43), but no better, and tying degradation to land use made it worse. The lattice's tipping is too sharp for the dry northern fringe, where it collapses while the satellites still see sparse shrubs. Adding seed rain from outside the lattice, the Sahel's version of the coral reef's missing larvae, stopped the dry edge collapsing. Tested from 1982 to 2021 against NOAA's AVHRR record, through the droughts and the recovery, the lattice now matches rain on level, trend and recovery and does slightly better on each site's year-to-year changes (0.26 against 0.19), since its cover carries memory of past years as shrubs do. Tipping itself still can't be told apart from rain in the model fits. Testing it in the data directly, as land that doesn't come back when the rain does, soil mattered most (sandier sites recovered less), returning rain didn't decide which sites recovered, and heavy land use blunted the response to rain, clearly so by 2015-21: the pattern a land-use-driven failure to recover would leave. Fakara itself doesn't stand out at the 8 km scale of AVHRR; its decline was measured on the ground. The real vegetation is more clumped than the lattice, by about 40% on uniform ground and still 22-34% with hollows that collect run-off.

    ERA5-Land describes the Po Valley in boxes about 9 km across. Gridded station products such as E-OBS fill the map by interpolating stations, and in doing so they no longer return the station readings themselves. This project builds daily maximum temperature on a 1 km grid for June to August 2021-2023 under two constraints. Between stations the surface obeys a reduced heat-budget equation, and at stations it equals the observation exactly. The method was built and checked first on simulated fields with a known truth, then run on real data: ERA5-Land, MODIS land surface temperature, terrain and land cover from Earth Engine, and 38 Meteostat stations.

    Temperature is reduced to sea level, θ = T + Γz with Γ = 6.5 K/km. Each day the model solves a steady heat budget, where u is the ERA5-Land wind, κ a mixing coefficient, θ_s the MODIS land surface temperature, τ_s(x) how quickly the air takes up the ground's temperature (set by the land-cover fractions f_c), θ_E the ERA5-Land temperature with relaxation time τ_a, and q(x) the heating the equation leaves out:

    u · ∇θ − κ ∇²θ + (θ − θ_s) / τ_s(x) + (θ − θ_E) / τ_a = q(x), 1 / τ_s(x) = Σ_c f_c(x) / τ_c

    Discretised, this is a sparse system Aθ = b + q. With H picking out the station cells and y the readings, the anchored surface uses the smallest and smoothest q that makes the surface equal every station, where G = HA⁻¹ needs one extra (adjoint) solve per station and W penalises size and roughness:

    q = q̂ + W⁻¹Gᵀ (G W⁻¹ Gᵀ)⁻¹ (y − Hθ₀), θ₀ = A⁻¹(b + q̂)

    The physics-informed network is a U-Net trained on the stations S, with the same heat budget as a penalty at every land cell Ω, weighted by λ:

    loss = (1/|S|) Σ_S (θ̂_s − y_s)² + pinball(5%, 95%) + λ (1/|Ω|) Σ_Ω (Aθ̂ − b)²

    Every model was scored at stations held out in 50 km blocks in 2023, after fitting on 2021-2022. The main results on the real data:

    • Every regional model beats ERA5-Land. RMSE falls from 1.89 K for lapse-corrected ERA5-Land to 1.60-1.62 K for regression-kriging, the physics model and the physics-informed network, about a sixth of ERA5-Land's error. These three are within 0.05 K of each other, and 38 stations cannot separate them.
    • The heat budget helps the network. Adding it to the loss takes the U-Net from 1.71 K to 1.60 K (λ = 0.3). With the heaviest weight (λ = 3) and anchored to the stations, it is the best model on the hottest 5% of days, at 1.60 K against 2.05 K for ERA5-Land and 1.87 K for kriging.
    • The anchoring holds. On all 276 days the surface returns every station reading to within 10⁻¹² K.

    The other results:

    • The physics reaches only a few kilometres. The fitted relaxation time is about 1.2 hours, so a station's influence on the map runs about 7 km downwind on a median day, 4 km on the calmest and 14 km on the windiest. Beyond that the statistical prior has to carry the information.
    • The XGBoost prior does not carry over. On the simulated data, XGBoost learning the missing heating from land cover and terrain gave the best model (0.79 K). On the real data it made the anchored model worse (1.61 to 1.75 K).
    • The U-Net emulator smooths the extremes. Trained to reproduce the physics surface from coarse maps, it comes close overall (1.65 K) but scores 1.85 K on the hottest days.
    • Above 800 m no model improves on lapse-corrected ERA5-Land (1.74 K), as a fixed lapse rate is used everywhere.

    We could separate the best models with the regional ARPA station networks, which have about ten times as many stations. A lapse rate fitted each day and a second-order advection scheme, so that the fitted mixing coefficient can be read physically, would improve the physics itself.

    6. 06A U-Net emulator of the physics

      The usual way to put a neural network into downscaling is to train it on a dense target. It has 120,000 parameters and is written in JAX.

      Its target is the anchored physics surface, built for each fold without that fold's stations, so it learns to reproduce the physics model from coarse inputs alone. This is an emulator, not a fair contest with the physics, and it shows how much of the anchored surface can be predicted from the coarse fields.

      • Hottest 5% of days. 1.85 K against 1.67 K. An emulator smooths its target, and the extremes are what get smoothed away.
      • Intervals. Its 90% interval held 63% of real 2023 readings. Conformalised quantile regression (Romano, Patterson & Candès, 2019) widens it by the 90th percentile of the 2022 misses, which on the real data overshoots to 95% with a mean width of 6.6 K (86% and 3.0 K on the simulated check).

    7. 07The heat budget in the network's loss

      A fair comparison trains the network on the thermometers themselves. The same U-Net was trained on the stations with the heat-budget residual Aθ̂ − b as a penalty at every land cell, using the same stencil and fitted parameters as the physics model.     Inspired by Ecology for Bankers, in which Robert May, Simon Levin and George Sugihara asked what bankers could learn from ecologists (Nature, February 2008, seven months before Lehman Brothers collapsed). The main piece is a 16-page LaTeX note deriving the tools they used. May's stability bound says a large web of interacting parts, species or firms, stays stable only while the strength and density of its links stay below a limit, σ√(NC) < 1. It comes from the circular law: the eigenvalues of a large random matrix fill a disc, and once that disc crosses zero the system tips. Percolation thresholds are the network version: if a failure passes along each link with some probability, there is a sharp point above which it spreads to a large share of the network instead of dying out. The wiring matters as well as the number of links. In disassortative networks, where hubs connect mostly to small nodes, as in payment systems and pollination webs, the threshold doubles for the same number of links per node, so weak shocks die out. Pushed past it, they fail more completely than a randomly wired network, so a network that looks stable in normal times can hide a worse collapse.

    The case study is supply chain risk when the Rhine runs low, as in 2018 and again in July 2026, when the Kaub gauge matched the 25 cm record. A network of 4,000 firms at Rhine-basin commercial hubs is calibrated so that a low-water month costs about 1% of German industrial output. That 1% does not pin the network down: economies anywhere from 6% to 89% of the way to their percolation threshold reproduce it, so the same observed loss fits a network that is safe and one close to tipping. What decides it is whether road and rail can take over from the barges; if they can't, the same low water costs about a third of output. The same low water is then modelled three ways, each firm simply disrupted or not, with identical failure chances: independently, with a standard one-factor correlation model, and through the network. In the worst 1% of months independent failures lose 1.8% of output, the network 2.3% and the correlation model 7.4%: one common shock makes the whole economy fail at once, while supply-chain dependence is local. Past the network's threshold the average loss itself jumps to 38%, which neither simpler model would predict from an ordinary year. Over a four-month episode, with firms staying down while they rebuild stocks, every model loses about 6% of one month's output, and the worst 1% runs from 8% (independent) through 10% (network) to 26% (correlated). The note also applies the same tools to structured credit.

    Seismology is built on signal processing, which ties it to my other projects, and earth systems are where my passion in applied mathematics lies. Small earthquakes under Los Angeles are often buried in traffic and other noise. I took 3,000 of the noisiest recordings (the bottom fifth by signal-to-noise) of small, local earthquakes from the Southern California Seismic Network, from a dataset where analysts have marked each P-wave arrival and its first motion. The P wave is the first wave to arrive from an earthquake. The time it lands at each station is used to locate the quake, and whether the ground first moves up or down at each station (the first motion) shows how the fault slipped. Earthquake catalogues and later reanalysis are built on these two numbers, so they are what any denoising has to preserve. With so many denoising methods available, the question was whether any of them can make these noisy recordings usable.

    I picked arrivals with an STA/LTA trigger, the standard picker in seismology, and calibrated it and a first-motion estimator on quiet recordings, where they agree with the analysts 99% and 97% of the time. On the noisy set I compared a bandpass filter, wavelet thresholding, a random-matrix-theory denoiser (a PCA template with a Marchenko-Pastur cut-off), and wavelets followed by random matrix theory. Random matrix theory looked best, picking 98% of arrivals, but only because every recording had its arrival at the same point in the window. With the arrivals moved at random it fell to 60%, against 56% with no denoising at all. Wavelets raised the signal-to-noise ratio by about 3 dB but flipped the first motion on about one trace in fourteen. A plain bandpass filter was the only method that helped (from 55% to 63% of arrivals picked) without changing any first motions.

    The dataset holds single-station recordings, so to test combining sensors I built a synthetic array: 24 sensors in a line, one wave crossing them, and noise from a road shared between neighbouring sensors. Minimum-variance (Capon) beamforming, which weights each sensor using the noise covariance, beats a simple average because it learns the shared road noise and cancels it. It only does so once the covariance is cleaned with shrinkage: raw weights need about 0.8 s of noise to estimate, cleaned ones work from 0.2 s.

    How the data flows
    1. Input. An HDF5 file of labelled waveforms: X (one row per recording, samples along the row), Y (label), mag, snr, dist and sncls (station code). Boolean masks keep CI-network recordings that are local (≤ 50 km), small (M ≤ 3.5) and in the lowest 20% of signal-to-noise.
    2. Denoise. The selected waveforms are stacked into a matrix (recordings × samples). Its correlation matrix is eigendecomposed, only eigenvalues above the Marchenko–Pastur noise edge are kept, and the matrix is rebuilt. Each trace is then wavelet-denoised (db4, 4 levels, soft threshold).
    3. Detect. The same STA/LTA picker runs on five versions of each record: raw, imported denoiser, delay-and-sum stack, optimal weights, and optimal weights with a cleaned covariance. Each gives a pick time and a detected or missed flag.
    4. Output. One table with a row per event per method, compared with the catalogue picks to give the completeness magnitude, pick bias and crossover window.

    In commodities the hedger who matters most is often the producer: a farm that knows roughly how much it will harvest but not what it will be paid. This project tests the standard producer hedges against one real shock. Russia had its worst heatwave on record through July 2010 and banned grain exports on 5 August, and European wheat, competing with Black Sea grain for the same buyers, rose 39%, from 136 euros a tonne on 3 May to 189 on 31 August. An Italian soft-wheat producer hedges on 3 May the crop it sells on 31 August, choosing between eight standard hedges: no hedge, selling all or half forward with futures, buying a put, a zero-cost collar (a put paid for by selling a call), a put spread, a three-way fence, and layering futures sales month by month. The options are priced by Black (1976), the same model used in the Asian option pricer (014). Euronext milling wheat isn't freely available for 2010, so CBOT wheat converted to euros stands in for it.

    In hindsight the producer did best unhedged, since a price spike is good news for a seller, and of the hedges the put and the put spread kept most of the rally while futures gave all of it up and the collar was capped at 145 euros. Judged on 3 May, before the event, the ranking reverses: by the average of the worst 5% of outcomes under the pricing model (the expected shortfall), short futures and the collar protected best and staying unhedged was the most dangerous. The right hedge depends on what the producer can't afford: a farm that must cover its costs should lock in a price, one that can stand a bad year but not a missed rally should buy puts. The other side of the market tells the opposite story. An Italian miller buying wheat saved €53 a tonne by buying futures, and €43-46 with a call or a zero-cost collar, while the cheaper call spread and three-way, capped at +15%, saved only €14 in a 39% rise. The same weather shock in another country was a windfall for one side and a loss for the other. Adding yield risk changes the producer's answer: when the harvest falls with the price rise, the best hedge drops from 100% of the expected crop to about half, and a drought-hit producer fully hedged in 2010 ended with €74 per tonne against €127 unhedged.     Undergraduate numerical simulation work in C++. Laplace's equation is solved on the unit square, with fixed values on the boundary and fixed-potential objects inside the domain, first with Dirichlet and then with Neumann boundary conditions. Successive over-relaxation (SOR) is an iterative method: each grid point is repeatedly replaced by the average of its four neighbours, as in Gauss-Seidel, but each update is pushed beyond that average by a relaxation factor between 1 and 2, which cuts the number of sweeps needed to converge many times over.

    I later ported the solver to Python with Numba to study the compute cost alongside the solution: how the number of sweeps depends on the relaxation factor and the grid size. The best factor climbs towards 2 as the grid refines, close to the theoretical 2 / (1 + sin(π/(N-1))); on a 253 x 253 grid the optimal factor converges in 760 sweeps against 4,047 at 1.8. The same SOR machinery was then applied to the Black-Scholes equation, where it matches the closed-form price to within 0.003. A companion lab solved the time-independent Schrödinger equation in one dimension with the Numerov method, for a finite well and a harmonic potential.
