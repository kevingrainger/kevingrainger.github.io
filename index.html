  • My undergraduate capstone at Trinity College Dublin, supervised by Prof. Cormac McGuinness: an attempt to model the electronic shell structure and spin patterns of metal-porphyrin nano-networks with density functional theory. Porphyrins are flat rings of four pyrroles that hold a metal atom at the centre. They are the core of haemoglobin (iron) and chlorophyll (magnesium), and their spin and charge can be tuned through the central metal and whatever binds to it, which makes them candidates for molecular electronics, catalysis and chemical sensing. On-surface synthesis can now fuse them into chains and ribbons, and the project carries on from earlier studies of these larger porphyrin systems. The calculations ran in FHI-aims, the Fritz Haber Institute's density functional theory code, which expands the electron orbitals in numeric atom-centred functions. DFT replaces the many-electron wavefunction with the electron density and solves the Kohn-Sham equations self-consistently.

    The main focus was how the bridge between units (weak, strong or fully fused), the central metal and the fused ligand arms combine to set the spin and the energy gap. The metals were zinc, iron, vanadium, cobalt and manganese, chosen to span the d shell from three electrons to ten. The bridge decides whether a bare chain is magnetic: weakly joined chains grow unpaired electrons on their carbon backbone, while fully fused ones pair them up. The metal matters through its d electrons: zinc's full shell leaves the carbon backbone on its own, while vanadium and iron put their d orbitals at the frontier. One oxygen bound to the metal overrides the bridge: every iron chain settles at S = 1 and every vanadium chain at S = ½, with the backbone spin gone and the gap still set by the bridge. A spin that switches when a molecule binds is the mechanism a chemical sensor needs.

  • A rebuilt agent-based model of Xylella spreading through an olive grove, carried from tree to tree by spittlebugs. I implemented a lighter version of the original model, checked against it, so that it can be run and tuned many more times. Outbreaks are usually fatal: 39 of 40 take the whole grove, half of it within about 2.3 years. I expected some kind of equilibrium, with dying trees slowing the spread, but none appeared: a tree takes about two years to show symptoms, which leaves plenty of time for the bacterium to reach the trees around it. A natural next step is multilevel Monte Carlo: most runs on a cheap, coarse version of the grove, and a few on progressively finer versions to correct what the coarsening gets wrong, so the full model is only paid for where it changes the answer.

    The aim was to bring in percolation theory and look for planting spacings and felling patterns that break up the spread. Two thresholds turned up. A spittlebug stays on a tree for about 18 days at a time. If its chance of passing on the infection during that stay is below roughly 16%, outbreaks die out; above about 29%, they take the grove. Spacing acts like a percolation threshold too: with the number of spittlebugs per tree held fixed, outbreaks stop taking the grove once trees are 6 to 8 cells apart. Many felling patterns were tried, varying the radius and how quickly infected trees are found, and none slowed the spread. A first twin experiment with a particle filter shows what data assimilation needs: counting dead trees from above leaves the infection rate almost unconstrained (anywhere from 0.1 to 0.9 after six years), because every outbreak above the threshold takes the whole grove. The images have to show which trees die and in what order. The next step is to fit that spatial pattern in real image sequences, like the Gallipoli pair, with a Wasserstein-distance loss and multilevel Monte Carlo to keep the runs affordable, before designing felling patterns.

    †Recreation of Fierro, Liccardo & Porcelli (2019), Scientific Reports.

    How the data flows
    1. Grove. A 90 × 90 NumPy array of cell types (shrub 0, tree 1, empty 2), with trees planted as 3 × 3 blocks every 5 cells. Each tree's health, infection day and symptom day are held in dicts keyed by tree position.
    2. Vectors. The spittlebugs are stored as parallel arrays with one entry per insect: x and y positions (int16), and infected and alive flags (bool). The paper's density is sampled down for speed and transmission scaled up to compensate.
    3. Time step. 48 steps per day. Each step, every insect moves to a neighbouring cell with probabilities set by the cell type and the season (tender sprouts before day 185, hard after). An insect on an infected tree may pick up the bacterium; an infected insect on a healthy tree may pass it on.
    4. Record. Each day, the S, I and R tree counts and the infected-insect fraction are appended to history lists. The transmission log adds one row per infection: source tree, target tree, day.
    5. Theory. From the log, transmissions are binned by distance to give T(d), and the front position is fitted against time to give its speed. These feed the percolation calculation and the front-speed formula, which return predicted outbreak size and a predicted timeline.
    6. Output. A three-panel figure (grove map, SIR curves with seasonal phases, insect infection rate), with the theory curves overlaid on the simulated ones.

  • This project models a reef much as physics models a magnet. Taking the Potts and Ising models as inspiration, it treats the reef as a lattice of interacting cells, a variation on cellular automata. Each square is healthy coral, bleached coral, bare rock or seaweed. Its interactions with its nearest and second-nearest neighbours are set to approximate real biological processes: coral spreading into free space nearby, grazing fish keeping seaweed down, and coral dying under prolonged heat. The results were mixed. Reefs get stuck: once heat kills enough coral, cooling the water back down does not bring it back. A mean-field model, where every patch feels every other, gets stuck about 60% worse than a lattice where each patch feels only its four nearest neighbours. Warming speed barely mattered, and there were very few early-warning signals before the whole reef collapsed. I'd like to look at that further once the model is tuned to real reef data.

    The next step was real data: how much heat can Scott Reef's corals take? The model now runs on NOAA's degree heating weeks (DHW), a measure of heat built up over weeks, and each colony gets its own heat tolerance, similar between neighbours (a random-field Potts model). It is fitted by simulation-based inference to 65 bleaching surveys of Scott Reef in April 2016 by AIMS, the Australian Institute of Marine Science. What agrees with the data: once colonies differ in tolerance, the model reproduces the April surveys. What the data can't settle: two very different reefs reproduce them equally well, a sensitive one that bleaches from about 4 DHW (NOAA's alert level) and a tolerant one that holds out to about 13 DHW. A single survey from February or March would tell them apart, and none exists. What points one way: AIMS reported widespread coral death at Scott Reef in early April, which only the sensitive reef predicts. The next phase of this project is better data: earlier and denser surveys of the reef.

  • This continues the theme of earth systems as lattices, and their links back to physics. Earth-observation data comes as rasters, plants interact with their neighbours, and soil health and soil moisture behave like underlying fields, so the parallels with lattice models in physics are worth exploring. Desertification is a pressing climate problem I have always been interested in, and Kéfi et al.'s 2007 Nature paper on vegetation patterns and imminent desertification inspired me to look at it this way. The model is their probabilistic cellular automaton: each cell is vegetated, empty but fertile, or degraded, and bare soil recovers faster next to vegetation (facilitation), the same neighbour coupling as the coral reef's Potts model. With strong facilitation the system shows hysteresis: recovering from desert takes much more rainfall than the collapse did. The mean-field theory, which ignores space, overstates how stuck it gets.

    The patch structure gives a much clearer early warning than total cover. As rainfall falls, the largest patch drops from 90% of all vegetation to 6% while cover is still near a half, well before the collapse, so tracking patch sizes in satellite imagery would warn of decline where average greenness would not. I drove the lattice with real rainfall at two Sahel sites: Gourma in Mali, which re-greened after the droughts, and Fakara in Niger, which did not. The rainfall data was a problem. Sahel rain falls in short, local storms, and averaging it over large grid boxes damps the signal: ERA5 came out about 35% too dry and agreed poorly with IMERG, so the runs use CHIRPS, which blends satellite estimates with rain gauges. As a stress test, the 1980s drought takes the lattice just below its tipping point, but too briefly to collapse it. Rainfall also predicted re-greening and decline less well than I expected: at Fakara greenness hardly follows the rain, and land use matters more.

    Played year by year as a strip of landscape beside each site's satellite record, the lattice loses about a quarter of its cover through the 1980s at both sites; MODIS greenness follows it loosely at Gourma and not at all at Fakara. Over 2000-2025 the lattice gets the direction of change right at Gourma but too weakly (+2.8% a decade against +9.3% in MODIS), and drifts down at Fakara where the satellites see none. Work in progress: the covariates behind land being lost and coming back, such as grazing, cropping and soil.

  • In my work in quantitative climate risk at Wieldmore I became interested in regional climate models: smaller, region-specific models of a mesoclimate, especially for Europe's industrial belts. A region like the Po Valley is small enough to put a PDE mesh and machine learning on directly, and it sits between two rich sources: ERA5 and Copernicus gridded data, and a dense network of weather stations. The interesting question is what you give up when you leave a physically continuous system like ERA5. Once you cut out a region and enforce boundaries, you are at the mercy of the boundary conditions. The aim was a calibrated model of daily maximum temperature for the Po Valley that matches every station reading exactly.

    Three approaches were compared against ERA5-Land and the standard statistical method, regression with kriged residuals. First, physics: a simplified heat-budget equation solved on a 1 km grid, with ERA5-Land on the boundary and satellite surface temperature heating the air from below, then adjusted so the surface passes exactly through every station reading. XGBoost is used to bridge the physical equation and the anchored station points. Second, a U-Net, the standard image-to-image network in downscaling, trained to reproduce the physics surface from coarse maps alone. Third, a physics-informed U-Net trained on the stations themselves, whose loss adds the error in the heat-budget equation at every grid cell to the error at the stations, so the physics fills the gaps between thermometers.

    Built and checked first on simulated fields with a known truth, it has now run on the real data: ERA5-Land, MODIS, Earth Engine terrain and 38 stations, scored at stations held out in 50 km blocks in 2023. Every regional model beats ERA5-Land by about 0.3 K (1.89 K down to about 1.60 K), and the best ones, kriging, the physics and the physics-informed network, are level with each other. The physics earns its place in the network: the heat budget in the loss takes it from 1.71 K to 1.60 K, and anchored to the stations it is the best model on the hottest days. Next are the denser ARPA station networks, to separate the best models, and better benchmarking against ERA5 alone.

    How the data flows

    The code runs end to end on simulated daily fields of the same shape; the download script for the real products ships with it.

    1. Input. ERA5-Land daily maximum temperature, skin temperature and afternoon wind at 0.1°; MODIS Aqua daytime land surface temperature at 1 km; elevation and six land-cover fractions per 1 km cell; station daily maxima.
    2. Daily fields. Everything is reduced to sea level with a 6.5 K/km lapse rate. Cloud gaps in the MODIS field are filled with ERA5-Land skin temperature plus each cell's mean clear-sky offset for the month.
    3. Physics. A steady advection-diffusion-relaxation equation is assembled as a sparse system and factorised once per day. Its relaxation times, one per land-cover class, are fitted with adjoint gradients.
    4. Anchor and learn. One adjoint solve per station gives its footprint. The smallest, smoothest forcing q that makes the surface equal every station is found in closed form; XGBoost learns q from land cover and terrain and supplies it as a prior.
    5. Learn. A U-Net emulates the anchored surface with conformal intervals; a physics-informed U-Net is trained on station readings with the heat-budget residual in its loss.
    6. Output. Daily maximum temperature at 1 km that returns every station reading exactly, a map of the heating the equation lacks, and a skill table from 50 km blocks held out in a year never used for fitting.

  • A fleet of solar plants carries correlated risk. If each site's P95, its output in a bad year, is worked out on its own, the fleet's bad year is underpriced, because the same clouds cover many sites at once. This project builds the framework for measuring that shared risk, the step that has to come before simulated weather or stochastic event sets can price it. Generation is proxied by ERA5 surface solar radiation for 25 Spanish solar regions in 2023, expressed as the clear-sky index: the share of the possible sunlight that gets through the cloud. The correlation between two sites falls with distance, and how far the clouds reach sets the scale: it drops to 1/e over about 350 km in winter, 440 km in spring and summer, and 670 km in autumn. In risk terms the portfolio of 25 sites behaves like only 3-4 independent ones.

    ECMWF runs two forecast models side by side: IFS, its physics model, and AIFS, an AI model trained on ERA5. Their daily solar forecasts for the sites, 1 to 7 days ahead, were scored against ERA5. For risk, the useful finding is that forecast errors are far more local than the weather itself: they decorrelate over about 170-230 km, against about 500 km for the weather. Next is to use both models to build a stochastic event set of correlated low-solar periods, add more widely spread sites in different weather systems, bring in power prices, and move to CERRA at 5.5 km, since ERA5's 31 km grid smooths out small clouds.

  • There is a large literature on weather and coffee prices, and this project tests how much weather moves both beans, arabica from Brazil and robusta from Vietnam, from an importer's point of view. Monthly World Bank prices for 2004-2026 are taken first with the standard driver stripped out, the Brazilian real, as gas was for Italian power. The weather comes from ERA5 for each origin: frost and three-month dryness in Brazil's arabica belt, heat and dryness in Vietnam's Central Highlands, and El Niño. Weather moves both beans in both regions: Vietnam heat has a partial correlation of about 0.17 with each bean, and of the hottest tenth of months in Vietnam, 32% were also top-10% price rises against 10% by chance. A Gumbel copula, which captures extremes arriving together, puts that tail dependence at about 0.15, while a Gaussian copula would say zero.

    The risk also compounds across origins. In the 16 months when both Brazil and Vietnam were hit at once, both beans rose about 3.5% a month, against roughly 1% when one origin was hit. The two beans spike together half the time (a Gumbel tail coefficient of 0.46, rising from about 0.31 in 2013 to 0.55 now), so buying from the other origin hedges an importer less and less when it matters, and a global hedge has to reflect joint weather risk. A Kalman filter on the arabica futures curve shows the market pricing Vietnam's 2024 heat into the convenience yield (a significant weather term, p = 0.004). Finally, does ECMWF's seasonal forecast help with the price? It beats climatology on Vietnam's weather (a skill score of 0.41 for heat), and fed through the weather-price relationship it lifts the importer's correct buy-or-wait calls from about 40% to 50% and does about 0.6-0.7 percentage points of the purchase price better per quarter than assuming a normal season. Its real value is in the tail: in the months when the forecast flagged Vietnam as unusually hot or dry, arabica rose 7.0% and robusta 5.7% over the next three months, against 1.4% and 0.9% on average, a result that holds outside the 2023-25 rally. For an importer, that is a signal worth hedging on.

  • The devastating storms in Chile got me interested in how Earth observation can both help anticipate events like these and show their impact on industrial zones. Heavy industry is a good subject because it can be measured in so many ways: a large plant footprint, shipping data, and heat from energy-intensive furnaces. Each gives a way to estimate production, and so a downstream view of where prices are likely to go, or why they went the way they did. Copper suits this well, because its supply chain is short and concentrated: concentrate leaves a handful of Chilean and Peruvian ports, and it is smelted in a few dozen plants. Using Sentinel-2's shortwave infrared bands I detect the thermal signatures of smelter furnaces, and ship traffic at the copper ports comes from IMF PortWatch, built from the AIS position beacons that large ships carry. Ports that ship forestry, salmon and fishmeal serve as placebos.

    The project was fairly successful at measuring supply. Dry-bulk exports from Peru's copper ports track Peru's official copper exports (correlation 0.67, against 0.27 for the placebo ports), and exports from the port serving Grasberg fell 89% after the September 2025 mudslide. Smelter heat, from 9,900 Sentinel-2 passes over 24 sites since 2019, is detectable at 24 of 30 smelters.   • Inspired by Ecology for Bankers, in which Robert May, Simon Levin and George Sugihara asked what bankers could learn from ecologists (Nature, February 2008, seven months before Lehman Brothers collapsed). The main piece is a 16-page LaTeX note deriving the tools they used. May's stability bound says a large web of interacting parts, species or firms, stays stable only while the strength and density of its links stay below a limit, σ√(NC) < 1. It comes from the circular law: the eigenvalues of a large random matrix fill a disc, and once that disc crosses zero the system tips. Percolation thresholds are the network version: if a failure passes along each link with some probability, there is a sharp point above which it spreads to a large share of the network instead of dying out. The wiring matters as well as the number of links. In disassortative networks, where hubs connect mostly to small nodes, as in payment systems and pollination webs, the threshold doubles for the same number of links per node, so weak shocks die out. Pushed past it, they fail more completely than a randomly wired network, so a network that looks robust in normal times can hide a worse collapse.

    The case study is supply chain risk when the Rhine runs low, as in 2018 and again in July 2026, when the Kaub gauge matched the 25 cm record. A network of 4,000 firms at Rhine-basin commercial hubs is calibrated so that a low-water month costs about 1% of German industrial output. That 1% does not pin the network down: economies anywhere from 6% to 89% of the way to their percolation threshold reproduce it, so the same observed loss fits a network that is safe and one close to tipping. What decides it is whether road and rail can take over from the barges; if they can't, the same low water costs about a third of output. The same low water is then modelled three ways, each firm simply disrupted or not, with identical failure chances: independently, with a standard one-factor correlation model, and through the network. In the worst 1% of months independent failures lose 1.8% of output, the network 2.3% and the correlation model 7.4%: one common shock makes the whole economy fail at once, while supply-chain dependence is local. Past the network's threshold the average loss itself jumps to 38%, which neither simpler model would predict from an ordinary year. Over a four-month episode, with firms staying down while they rebuild stocks, every model loses about 6% of one month's output, and the worst 1% runs from 8% (independent) through 10% (network) to 26% (correlated). The note also applies the same tools to structured credit.

  • Seismology is built on signal processing, which ties it to my other projects, and earth systems are where my passion in applied mathematics lies. Small earthquakes under Los Angeles are often buried in traffic and other noise. I took 3,000 of the noisiest recordings (the bottom fifth by signal-to-noise) of small, local earthquakes from the Southern California Seismic Network, from a dataset where analysts have marked each P-wave arrival and its first motion. The P wave is the first wave to arrive from an earthquake. The time it lands at each station is used to locate the quake, and whether the ground first moves up or down at each station (the first motion) shows how the fault slipped. Earthquake catalogues and later reanalysis are built on these two numbers, so they are what any denoising has to preserve. With so many denoising methods available, the question was whether any of them can make these noisy recordings usable.

    I picked arrivals with an STA/LTA trigger, the standard picker in seismology, and calibrated it and a first-motion estimator on quiet recordings, where they agree with the analysts 99% and 97% of the time. On the noisy set I compared a bandpass filter, wavelet thresholding, a random-matrix-theory denoiser, and wavelets followed by random matrix theory. Random matrix theory looked best, picking 98% of arrivals, but only because every recording had its arrival at the same point in the window. With the arrivals moved at random it fell to 60%, against 56% with no denoising at all. Wavelets raised the signal-to-noise ratio by about 3 dB but flipped the first motion on about one trace in fourteen. A plain bandpass filter was the only method that helped (from 55% to 63% of arrivals picked) without changing any first motions.

    The dataset holds single-station recordings, so to test combining sensors I built a synthetic array: 16 sensors, one wave crossing them, and noise from a road shared between neighbouring sensors. Minimum-variance (Capon) beamforming, which weights each sensor using the noise covariance, beats a simple average because it learns the shared road noise and cancels it. It only does so once the covariance is cleaned with shrinkage: raw weights need about 0.8 s of noise to estimate, cleaned ones work from 0.2 s.

  • Undergraduate numerical simulation work in C++. Laplace's equation is solved on the unit square, with fixed values on the boundary and fixed-potential objects inside the domain, first with Dirichlet and then with Neumann boundary conditions. Successive over-relaxation (SOR) is an iterative method: each grid point is repeatedly replaced by the average of its four neighbours, as in Gauss-Seidel, but each update is pushed beyond that average by a relaxation factor between 1 and 2, which cuts the number of sweeps needed to converge many times over.

    I later ported the solver to Python with Numba to study the compute cost alongside the solution: how the number of sweeps depends on the relaxation factor and the grid size. The best factor climbs towards 2 as the grid refines, close to the theoretical 2 / (1 + sin(π/(N-1))); on a 253 x 253 grid the optimal factor converges in 760 sweeps against 4,047 at 1.8. The same SOR machinery was then applied to the Black-Scholes equation, where it matches the closed-form price to within 0.003. A companion lab solved the time-independent Schrödinger equation in one dimension with the Numerov method, for a finite well and a harmonic potential.
