  • Aim: test whether coral reefs have tipping points, a level of heat past which they collapse and do not come back. A Potts lattice model, the statistical-physics model of magnetism adapted to reef states, is run by Markov chain Monte Carlo and calibrated to 23 years of Scott Reef surveys and satellite heat stress with Approximate Bayesian Computation. It then measures hysteresis, cusp surfaces and early warning signals as the range of interaction between reef patches changes.

    †Builds on Mumby, Hastings & Edwards (2007). Survey data from Gilmour, Cook & Ryan (2021).

    Python · Potts model · Approximate Bayesian Computation · Earth Engine

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Calibrate to the reef

      Fit the lattice to 23 years of Scott Reef coral cover and satellite heat stress using Approximate Bayesian Computation: try many parameter sets and keep those whose simulated reef looks like the real one.

    2. 02Switch the interaction range

      Each site feels only its z nearest neighbours, with z = 0, 4, 8, 12 or every site. Coupling is divided by z, so total coupling stays fixed while the range changes.

    3. 03Ramp the heat up, then down

      Raise heat stress until the reef collapses, then lower it again. If recovery needs much cooler water than collapse did, the gap is the hysteresis width W(z).

    4. 04Sweep heat and coupling

      Map equilibrium cover over a grid of heat stress and coupling to draw the cusp surface, and test whether early warning signals rise before each transition.

    5. 05Change the ramp rate

      Warm the reef to a level it could survive if warmed slowly, at increasing speeds, to find the critical rate and check whether the warnings still fire.

    How the data flows
    1. Input. A daily heat-stress series and the Scott Reef coral-cover survey, each a 1D array indexed by date. The current repo version reads daily temperature and observed bleached fraction from Coral temps.xlsx (columns temp and smooth) with pandas.
    2. State. The reef is a 2D integer NumPy array (100 × 100), one cell per patch, each holding a state code: healthy, bleached, bare rock or ocean. A float array of the same shape holds each cell's accumulated heat stress.
    3. Update. Each simulated day, the Metropolis step proposes a new state for each cell, computes the energy change from its neighbours (coupling J divided by the range z) and the local heat stress, and accepts the change with probability exp(−β ΔE).
    4. Record. After each day, the count of cells in each state is appended to a dict of lists (healthy, bleached, ...), giving one time series per state.
    5. Calibrate. ABC draws many parameter sets, runs the model for each, compares its cover series with the survey, and keeps the closest sets.
    6. Output (planned). The calibrated model is rerun across the sweeps (range z, heat ramps, a heat × coupling grid, ramp rates). Each run is reduced to a few numbers, such as hysteresis width, warning onset, tipped or survived, stored as one row per run in a table that the figures are drawn from.

  • Aim: predict how far and how fast Xylella spreads through an olive grove, and how far apart trees must be planted to stop it. A stochastic agent-based SIR simulation recreates the published model, with spittlebugs as the disease vector and seasonal infection patterns. The extension maps the simulation onto bond percolation, so the final outbreak size and the critical planting distance follow analytically, with a front-speed argument for the timeline.

    †Recreation of Fierro, Liccardo & Porcelli (2019), Scientific Reports. Percolation mapping from Grassberger (1983) and Newman (2002).

    Python · agent-based modelling · percolation theory

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Rebuild the source model

      Re-run the Fierro, Liccardo and Porcelli grove: 256 trees and spittlebugs moving between them over three years. The logic is unchanged; a log now records which tree infected which, and on which day.

    2. 02Measure how infection travels

      From one logged run, measure the transmissibility T(d): the chance an infected tree passes the disease to a tree at distance d. Also measure how fast the infection front moves.

    3. 03Predict outbreak size without simulating

      A stochastic SIR epidemic ends in the same pattern as bond percolation: link each pair of trees with probability T(d), and the outbreak is the cluster containing the first tree. This gives the final size and the critical planting distance directly.

    4. 04Predict the timeline

      Percolation has no clock. A front moving at constant speed gives quadratic growth in infected trees, and the yearly reset of the vector population turns that curve into a staircase.

    5. 05Check one prediction

      Run a full simulation at the predicted critical spacing to confirm it.

    How the data flows
    1. Grove. A 90 × 90 NumPy array of cell types (shrub 0, tree 1, empty 2), with trees planted as 3 × 3 blocks every 5 cells. Each tree's health, infection day and symptom day are held in dicts keyed by tree position.
    2. Vectors. The spittlebugs are stored as parallel arrays with one entry per insect: x and y positions (int16), and infected and alive flags (bool). The paper's density is sampled down for speed and transmission scaled up to compensate.
    3. Time step. 48 steps per day. Each step, every insect moves to a neighbouring cell with probabilities set by the cell type and the season (tender sprouts before day 185, hard after). An insect on an infected tree may pick up the bacterium; an infected insect on a healthy tree may pass it on.
    4. Record. Each day, the S, I and R tree counts and the infected-insect fraction are appended to history lists. The transmission log adds one row per infection: source tree, target tree, day.
    5. Theory. From the log, transmissions are binned by distance to give T(d), and the front position is fitted against time to give its speed. These feed the percolation calculation and the front-speed formula, which return predicted outbreak size and a predicted timeline.
    6. Output. A three-panel figure (grove map, SIR curves with seasonal phases, insect infection rate), with the theory curves overlaid on the simulated ones.

  • Aim: tools for when a single correlation number misleads. Covers tail dependence, the loss of correlation at high sampling frequency (the Epps effect), cleaning noisy correlation matrices with random matrix theory, and minimum-variance weighting, the same formula that combines many sensors into one signal. Used by the seismic and solar projects.

    †Builds on Epps (1979), Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters (1999) and Capon (1969). Full list in the pipeline tab.

    Python · NumPy · random matrix theory

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Choose what to measure

      Linear (Pearson), rank, tail, lead-lag and partial correlation answer different questions, so the library keeps them separate rather than reporting one number.

    2. 02Estimate from irregular ticks

      Correlation between two prices fades as you sample them faster, an artefact called the Epps effect. The Hayashi–Yoshida estimator works on the raw timestamps and avoids it.

    3. 03Separate signal from noise

      With N variables and T observations, pure noise spreads eigenvalues over a predictable band (Marchenko–Pastur). Eigenvalues above the band are signal; the rest are clipped or shrunk. Tyler's estimator handles heavy-tailed data.

    4. 04Use the cleaned matrix

      Count the independent modes (effective rank) and compute minimum-variance weights. The same formula gives optimal sensor weights in beamforming, which the seismic and solar projects import.

    How the data flows
    1. Input. A NumPy array X with one row per variable and one column per time step. For tick data, one pair of arrays per asset instead: timestamps and values, left unaligned.
    2. Measure. Each measure (Pearson, rank, tail, lead-lag, partial) is a function that returns an N × N matrix. The Hayashi–Yoshida estimator works directly on the unaligned timestamps, so nothing is resampled.
    3. Clean. From the matrix and q = N / T, compute the Marchenko–Pastur noise band. Cleaners return a new N × N matrix with the noise eigenvalues clipped or shrunk; Tyler's estimator replaces the sample matrix for heavy-tailed data.
    4. Use. Effective rank returns one number. Optimal weights return a length-N vector summing to 1. Array gain compares the noise of the weighted stack with the √N line expected from plain averaging.
    5. Output. Plain arrays and numbers, so other projects import the modules directly: the seismic denoiser uses stacking and cleaning, the Spanish solar project uses cleaning, effective rank and loadings.

  • Aim: detect smaller earthquakes in noisy urban seismometer records. Random matrix theory and wavelet thresholding recover P-wave onsets from labelled Southern California waveforms. The next stage scores five detection methods on one test, from the field-standard STA/LTA trigger to optimally weighted array stacking.

    2. 02Act 1: one trace

      STA/LTA compares the short-term average energy of a trace with its long-term average and triggers when the ratio jumps. Run it on the raw trace (M1) and on the imported denoiser's output (M2).

    How the data flows

    Steps 1–2 are the code in the repo now; steps 3–4 are the planned scoreboard.

    1. Input. An HDF5 file of labelled waveforms: X (one row per recording, samples along the row), Y (label), mag, snr, dist and sncls (station code). Boolean masks keep CI-network recordings that are local (≤ 50 km), small (M ≤ 3.5) and in the lowest 20% of signal-to-noise.
    2. Denoise. The selected waveforms are stacked into a matrix (recordings × samples). Its correlation matrix is eigendecomposed, only eigenvalues above the Marchenko–Pastur noise edge are kept, and the matrix is rebuilt. Each trace is then wavelet-denoised (db4, 4 levels, soft threshold).
    3. Detect. The same STA/LTA picker runs on five versions of each record: raw, imported denoiser, delay-and-sum stack, optimal weights, and optimal weights with a cleaned covariance. Each gives a pick time and a detected or missed flag.
    4. Output. One table with a row per event per method, compared with the catalogue picks to give the completeness magnitude, pick bias and crossover window.

  • Aim: price European, American and Asian options when volatility changes over time. Finite-difference PDE solvers using successive over-relaxation (SOR) are calibrated to real SPX option chains and validated against exact formulas, binomial trees and Monte Carlo, with a measured convergence order.

    †Asian method from Večeř (2001), after Rogers & Shi (1995). Projected SOR as in Wilmott, Howison & Dewynne (1995).

    Python · finite difference · projected SOR · Monte Carlo

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Read the market

      From the SPX chain of 4 January 2023, put-call parity gives the forward price and the discount factor. The implied interest rate is checked against January 2023 rates.

    2. 02Let volatility depend on time

      Read forward volatility σ(t) off the chain. European prices then depend only on the integrated variance, so an exact formula remains to test the solver against.

    3. 03Solve and validate

      Solve the PDE on a log-price grid with SOR, compare European prices with the exact formula, and fit the convergence order.

    4. 04American and Asian options

      American options need a free boundary, found by projected SOR and checked against a binomial tree. Asian options reduce to one dimension (Večeř) and are checked by Monte Carlo with a control variate. Asian pricing and SET50 are in progress.

    How the data flows

    Planned structure; Asian pricing and the SET50 calibration are still being built.

    1. Input. One day's option chain (SPX, 4 January 2023) as a table with one row per contract: strike, expiry, call or put, bid and ask.
    2. Extract. For each expiry, calls and puts at the same strike are paired, and a put-call parity regression gives the forward price and discount factor. Implied volatilities give total variance per expiry, and differencing that gives σ(t) as a piecewise function.
    3. Solve. The price lives on a 2D NumPy grid (log-price × time), solved backwards from the payoff with SOR. For American options, each sweep also takes the maximum of the value and the payoff, which finds the exercise boundary.
    4. Check. The same contracts are priced by the exact formula, a binomial tree and Monte Carlo. The error against grid size gives the convergence order.
    5. Output. Price surfaces as arrays, the exercise boundary as one price per time step, and a convergence table.

  • Aim: forecast Italian electricity price volatility a season ahead. Replicates a GARCH-MIDAS model linking volatility to extreme weather, then replaces observed weather with ECMWF seasonal forecasts and measures how many months ahead the forecast still adds skill.

    †Replication of Guerzoni, Riso & Zoia (2026), extended with SEAS5 forecasts. GARCH-MIDAS from Engle, Ghysels & Sohn (2013).

    Python · GARCH-MIDAS · ERA5 · SEAS5

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Gather prices and weather

      Italian day-ahead prices (GME), observed weather (ERA5) and 25-member seasonal forecasts (SEAS5).

    2. 02Replicate the paper

      GARCH-MIDAS splits daily variance into a slow level times a fast GARCH part. The slow level is a weighted average of past monthly weather extremes.

    3. 03Swap in forecasts

      Replace observed weather with SEAS5 forecasts made in advance, bias-corrected by quantile mapping, so the model can be used before the season happens.

    4. 04Score out of sample

      Score three models on the same unseen window: no weather, observed weather, forecast weather. The result is the forecast lead, in months, at which the advantage disappears.

    Results are added here as each run is finished.

    How the data flows

    Planned structure.

    1. Input. GME daily day-ahead prices, ERA5 daily weather over Italy, and SEAS5 25-member monthly forecasts, aligned into one table indexed by date.
    2. Features. Daily log returns of price. Monthly weather-extreme indices (one value per month), computed the same way from ERA5 and from each SEAS5 member after quantile-mapping bias correction.
    3. Fit. Three models on the same training window: GARCH(1,1) on returns alone, GARCH-MIDAS with ERA5 extremes, and GARCH-MIDAS with forecast extremes. Each outputs a daily variance forecast.
    4. Score. On the test window, each forecast is compared with realised variance using QLIKE and MSE, and a Diebold–Mariano test checks whether the differences are significant.
    5. Output. A table of losses per model per forecast lead. The skill horizon is the lead at which the forecast model stops beating the no-weather model.

  • Aim: spread solar capacity across Spain so that sites do not all generate, and depress the price, at the same time. Output is built from ERA5 weather, spatial correlation is modelled by distance and season and cleaned with random matrix theory, and five allocation rules are back-tested on revenue and worst year.

    †Weather from ERA5 (Hersbach et al. 2020); prices from ENTSO-E. Capture-rate framing from Hirth (2013).

    Python · ERA5 · ENTSO-E · corrlib

    View on GitHub
    1. 01Build generation from weather

      Turn hourly ERA5 radiation at 20–40 sites into output with a deliberately simple panel model, then remove the clear-sky profile so only the weather-driven deviations remain.

    2. 02Measure how sites move together

      Correlation falls with distance between sites. The decay length L is estimated by season, since winter blocking highs are expected to make diversification fail.

    3. 03Clean and find structure

      Clean the site matrix with corrlib, count its independent modes, and compute each site's capture rate: the price it actually earns, weighted by when it generates.

    4. 04Allocate and score

      Five allocation arms, from equal weight to a capture-rate objective, scored on revenue and worst year. Equal weight is expected to be competitive and is reported as such.

    How the data flows

    Planned structure.

    1. Input. Hourly ERA5 radiation and temperature at 20–40 site locations (a table of hours × sites), and hourly ENTSO-E day-ahead prices for Spain.
    2. Generation. A simple panel model turns each site's weather into hourly output. The clear-sky profile is subtracted, leaving only weather-driven deviations, in the same hours × sites shape.
    3. Correlation. Site × site correlation of the deviations, by season. Fitting ρ(d) = exp(−d / L) against distance gives the decay length L; the matrix is cleaned with corrlib.
    4. Structure. Effective rank of the cleaned matrix, each site's loading on the shared mode, and each site's capture rate: its output-weighted average price divided by the plain average price.
    5. Output. Five allocation arms, each a vector of capacity shares, back-tested over the years into one table of revenue and worst-year results.
